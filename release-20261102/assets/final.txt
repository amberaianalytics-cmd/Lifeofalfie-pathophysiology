Case 1: Coach Mando’s Morning Crunch (Osteoarthritis)
Dr. Rao adjusted his spectacles as the clinic door swung open. "Alright team," he said, "today
is musculoskeletal day. Be ready — bones may not lie, but joints sure love to complain."
Alfie, notebook in hand, grinned nervously. "Let’s do this."
Tina rolled her shoulders. "As long as we don’t talk about Coach Mando’s knees again."
"Too late," Lin replied, motioning toward the waiting room where Coach Mando sat with
two knee braces and a bucket of ice.
Ashwin yawned. "Bet it’s osteoarthritis again. Or maybe he joined a breakdancing
competition."
Alfie looked up, hesitated, and then said, "Dr. Rao, before we begin… my cousin would like to

Dr. Rao raised an eyebrow. "This is a medical setting, Alfie. He’ll be addressed by his given

---
Normal Physiology:
Skeletal Framework: Long bones are composed of cortical (compact) and trabecular
(spongy) bone. Joints — especially synovial joints — are lined with articular cartilage made
of hyaline cartilage.
Cartilage Integrity: Chondrocytes maintain matrix turnover (collagen II, proteoglycans).
Cartilage is avascular, relying on synovial fluid diffusion.

Case 2: Workbench Woes (Tendinitis vs. Bursitis)
Dr. Rao motioned to the next patient on the screen. "This one should be fun. Lin, read us the
chief complaint."
Lin peered down. "Elbow pain. Sharp, worsens with movement, especially when lifting his
coffee mug."
Ashwin leaned back. "Ah, the infamous barista’s bane."
Alfie flipped through anatomy notes. "We’re probably looking at lateral epicondylitis or
maybe olecranon bursitis."
Thomas raised a hand, quietly whispering, "Inflamed cushions... or rope tension at the
attachment?"
Dr. Rao nodded. "Good instincts. Let’s walk through the physiology."
---
Normal Physiology:
Tendons connect muscle to bone. They consist of dense regular connective tissue, rich in
collagen I, and are sparsely vascularized.
Bursae are synovial-fluid-filled sacs located at pressure points to reduce friction. Lined by
synovial membrane, they provide cushioning.
Inflammation of either can arise from overuse, trauma, or systemic inflammatory
conditions.
---
Pathophysiology:
In tendinitis, repetitive strain causes microtears in the tendon. The resulting collagen
disorganization triggers localized inflammation.
In bursitis, excess mechanical stress or infection leads to fluid accumulation, pain, and
limited mobility.
^^Tendinitis pain increases with active movement; bursitis worsens with pressure on the
area.^^

Case 3: Morning Lockjaw (Temporomandibular Joint Dysfunction)
Ashwin arrived late and clutching his jaw. "Don’t ask. I yawned too hard and now I can’t
close my mouth all the way."
Dr. Rao raised an eyebrow. "TMD — Temporomandibular Joint Dysfunction. Classic example
of overenthusiastic fatigue."
---
Normal Physiology:
The temporomandibular joint (TMJ) is a synovial joint formed between the mandibular
condyle and the temporal bone. It’s unique in its rotational and translational movements,
allowing jaw opening, chewing, and speaking.
Movement involves coordination of the masseter, temporalis, and lateral/medial pterygoid
muscles, controlled by CN V3 (mandibular branch of trigeminal nerve).
---
Pathophysiology:
Dysfunction arises from imbalance in muscle activity, articular disc displacement, bruxism,
or trauma. It may lead to inflammation, joint clicking, or pain with jaw movement.

Case 4: Fracture Fallout (Bone Remodeling)
Lin walked in with a patient’s chart. "Fell off a ladder. Tibial fracture. Clean break."
Dr. Rao tapped the whiteboard. "Good chance to review bone remodeling."
---
Normal Physiology:
Bone remodeling is a balance between osteoblast activity (bone deposition) and osteoclast
resorption. Controlled by hormones (PTH, calcitonin), mechanical stress, and cytokines.
Osteoblasts secrete osteoid and alkaline phosphatase, promoting mineralization.
Osteoclasts dissolve bone via acid and enzymes in a sealed resorption bay.
---
Pathophysiology:
In fractures, hematoma forms initially, followed by inflammation and soft callus formation
(fibrocartilage). Osteoblasts then form woven bone, replaced by stronger lamellar bone.
^^Fracture healing involves hematoma, soft callus, hard callus, and remodeling.^^

Dr. Rao nodded. "Nutrition, immobilization, and time. If all else fails, the body usually gets it

Case 6: Vertebral Compression Vexation (Osteoporosis)
Dr. Rao stood at the front of the small teaching clinic, flipping through a stack of lumbar
spine x-rays.
"We’ve got a fragile situation today — quite literally," he said, holding up an image.
"Alfie, what do you see here?"
Alfie squinted. "There’s a… wedge shape to the vertebra. Compression fracture?"
"Correct. This patient is a 70-year-old woman who slipped on wet tile. Minimal trauma.
Severe back pain. We’re dealing with osteoporosis."
Thomas, still adamant about being called Cousin Oracle, chimed in. "Could it be
osteopenia instead?"
Dr. Rao raised an eyebrow. "Good question. Let’s walk through it."
Normal Physiology:
• Bone remodeling is a continuous process balancing osteoblasts (bone formation)
and osteoclasts (bone resorption).
• Peak bone mass is achieved around age 30; after that, resorption tends to
outpace formation.
• Calcium homeostasis is tightly regulated by PTH, vitamin D (calcitriol),
and calcitonin.
• Estrogen helps inhibit osteoclast activity — hence, postmenopausal women are
at higher risk.
➡ Trabecular bone, found in vertebrae and hips, is particularly vulnerable to loss due to
high metabolic activity.
➡ DEXA (Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry) is used to measure bone mineral
density. T-score ≤ –2.5 = osteoporosis; between –1 and –2.5 = osteopenia.
Pathophysiology:
• In osteoporosis, bone resorption exceeds formation, leading to decreased bone
mass and microarchitectural deterioration.
• Silent until fracture — most common at vertebrae, hips, and wrists.
• Vertebral fractures often present with height loss, kyphosis, and back pain.

Case 7: Deep Vein Drama (DVT)
Story
Dr. Rao entered the room with his usual brisk pace. Alfie and the rest of the team were
already gathered around a patient lying on the exam table, clutching his calf.
“This is Mr. Levens,” said Alfie. “He flew in from London two days ago and now has sudden
left leg pain and swelling.”
“Classic presentation,” murmured Thomas, Alfie’s cousin, scribbling notes with an air of
prophecy. “Could it be… a clot?”
Dr. Rao arched an eyebrow. “Let’s review.”
Tina chimed in, “He’s got unilateral leg swelling, tenderness on palpation, and a faint bluish
hue.”
“And a positive Homan’s sign,” added Lin.
“^^Homan’s sign can be suggestive of DVT, but it's neither sensitive nor specific.^^” said Dr.
Rao. “Let’s dig in.”
Normal Physiology
Blood within veins flows under low pressure and is assisted by skeletal muscle contractions
and venous valves. The endothelium maintains a non-thrombogenic surface via secretion of
nitric oxide and prostacyclin.
Clot formation is normally prevented through three mechanisms:
→ Smooth endothelial surface (anti-adhesion)
→ Antithrombin III and Protein C/S activity (anticoagulants)
→ Laminar flow, preventing stasis
When homeostasis is disrupted, clotting cascades and platelet aggregation may initiate even
inappropriately.
Pathophysiology
Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) results from **Virchow’s Triad**:
→ **Stasis**: Immobility, long flights, surgery
→ **Endothelial injury**: Trauma, inflammation, catheterization
→ **Hypercoagulability**: Inherited (e.g., Factor V Leiden), acquired (e.g., malignancy,
pregnancy)

Case 8: Reactive Joint Woes (Reactive Arthritis)
Story
Dr. Rao waved a folder in the air. “Today’s case: a young man with sudden knee pain after a
recent bout of diarrhea.”
Ashwin leaned forward. “Weird. Any mention of eye issues or… weird urethral discharge?”
Alfie’s cousin, Thomas, whispered, “The triad speaks: joint, pee, and see.”
Tina rolled her eyes. “Thanks, Cousin Oracle.”
Dr. Rao nodded. “Classic post-infectious reactive arthritis.”
Normal Physiology
Reactive arthritis is an autoimmune condition that follows certain infections, particularly
gastrointestinal (e.g., Shigella, Salmonella, Yersinia, Campylobacter) or genitourinary (e.g.,
Chlamydia trachomatis).
Normally, the immune system mounts a localized response to infection, clearing pathogens
via cytokines, T cells, and antibodies.
In genetically susceptible individuals (especially HLA-B27+), immune responses can
become misdirected. Cross-reactivity between microbial and synovial antigens leads to
auto-inflammation in joints and other tissues.
Physiological immune regulation usually prevents this via peripheral tolerance and T-
regulatory cells, but failures here allow inflammation to extend to non-infected areas
(joints, eyes, urethra).
Pathophysiology
In reactive arthritis, inflammation is sterile — no live organisms are found in joint fluid.
Triggering infections often originate in the GI or GU tract. The immune system attacks
synovium and periarticular tissues due to molecular mimicry.
Cytokines like TNF-α, IL-17, and IL-23 drive inflammation, leading to synovial hypertrophy,
lymphocyte infiltration, and erosion.
^^Reactive arthritis presents with asymmetric oligoarthritis, often in lower extremities.^^
^^The triad includes arthritis, conjunctivitis, and urethritis.^^
^^HLA-B27 is associated with a higher risk and chronic progression.^^
Exam Hint Summary
• Think "Can't see, can't pee, can't climb a tree" — the triad of reactive arthritis.
• Often triggered by Chlamydia or GI pathogens.

Case 9: Systemic Weakness Spiral (Myasthenia Gravis vs. Lambert-Eaton)
Story
Thomas sat cross-legged, arms flopping. “I’m too weak to lift the truth.”
Dr. Rao raised an eyebrow. “Or just too lazy?”
Alfie grinned. “Could be either MG or LEMS.”
Tina said, “We covered this last week. MG hits the postsynaptic ACh receptors, right?”
Ashwin chimed in. “And LEMS hits the presynaptic calcium channels.”
Normal Physiology
Skeletal muscle contraction depends on proper neuromuscular junction (NMJ) function.
Presynaptic neurons release acetylcholine (ACh) into the synaptic cleft. ACh binds
postsynaptic nicotinic receptors on muscle fibers, triggering depolarization and contraction.
Presynaptic release is calcium-dependent — voltage-gated calcium channels allow influx
that triggers vesicle fusion.
In normal physiology:
• Adequate ACh is released upon each nerve impulse.
• Postsynaptic receptors respond with predictable strength.
• Muscle fatigue is minimal unless overstressed.
Pathophysiology
• In **Myasthenia Gravis (MG)**: Autoantibodies attack postsynaptic ACh receptors.
→ Reduced receptor availability → Weak signal → Rapid muscle fatigue.
→ Commonly affects ocular, bulbar muscles.
→ Improves with rest (ACh builds up).
^^Tensilon (edrophonium) test can temporarily improve symptoms.^^
• In **Lambert-Eaton Myasthenic Syndrome (LEMS)**: Autoantibodies target presynaptic
voltage-gated calcium channels.
→ Impaired ACh release → Weakness.
→ Paradoxical improvement with activity (more calcium accumulates).
• LEMS is often paraneoplastic (e.g., small cell lung cancer).
Exam Hint Summary
• MG: Postsynaptic issue. Worse with activity, improves with rest.
• LEMS: Presynaptic issue. Improves with activity.
• MG has ocular signs early (ptosis, diplopia).
• LEMS often linked to malignancy.
• EMG studies and antibody tests help differentiate.

Alfie whispered to Thomas, “Bet the kid’s immune system threw a tantrum.”
Thomas murmured, “And called it inflammation.”
Normal Physiology
In healthy children, immune tolerance prevents autoimmunity. T and B cells are regulated
by thymic selection and peripheral checks.
Synovial joints are normally lubricated by synovial fluid and protected by articular cartilage
and immune-privileged environments.
In JIA, these protections fail. Antigen-presenting cells activate T cells inappropriately,
leading to chronic inflammation.
Pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α drive joint swelling, pain, and
systemic features (in systemic subtype).
Pathophysiology
JIA includes several subtypes. **Systemic JIA** presents with spiking fevers, salmon-pink
rash, lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, and arthritis.
• Chronic synovial inflammation leads to pannus formation and joint destruction.
• Elevated ESR, CRP, ferritin, and sometimes ANA or RF.
• Requires differentiation from infections and malignancies.
^^Systemic JIA often mimics sepsis or leukemia in early presentation.^^
^^Look for quotidian fever pattern (same time daily).^^
^^IL-1 and IL-6 are key cytokines driving systemic features.^^
Exam Hint Summary
• JIA is diagnosed clinically — must be <16 years old and symptoms >6 weeks.
• Systemic subtype has fever, rash, and organ involvement.
• IL-1 blockers (anakinra) and IL-6 inhibitors (tocilizumab) are used.
• ANA can be positive but is not required.

Case 11: Fracture Friction Fiasco (Open Fracture with Fat Embolism
Syndrome)
Story
Dr. Rao adjusted his face shield as Alfie helped wheel in a patient from the ER. The man had
a visibly deformed femur, blood pooling around the dressing. Ashwin stared. "Is that bone
poking through?"
"Yep," Thomas nodded. "That’s what you call an open fracture."
Alfie grimaced. "His O2 is dropping, and he’s confused. Rash just popped up too."
"Keep him supine," Dr. Rao ordered. "We’re likely dealing with fat embolism syndrome."
Normal Physiology
Long bones like the femur contain yellow marrow rich in fat. When fractured, especially
with high-impact trauma, fat globules can enter torn venules.
Normal fracture healing involves hematoma formation, fibrocartilage callus, bony callus,
and remodeling over months.
Respiratory gas exchange occurs in alveoli. Any embolism (fat, air, clot) disrupts perfusion
and oxygenation, lowering PaO2.
Pathophysiology
In fat embolism syndrome (FES), marrow fat leaks into venous circulation, lodging in
pulmonary capillaries and sometimes systemic vessels.
Fat globules initiate inflammation (IL-1, TNF-α), leading to acute lung injury. Platelet
activation can cause petechial rash.
Neurologic symptoms stem from cerebral emboli. Symptoms classically appear 1–3 days
post-fracture.
Supportive care includes oxygen, IV fluids, and fracture stabilization.
Exam Hint Summary
^^Classic triad: hypoxemia, petechial rash, and neurologic symptoms after long bone
fracture^^
^^Fat embolism typically presents within 24–72 hours after injury^^
^^Treatment is supportive; steroids have unclear benefit^^

Case 12: Punched Patella Panic (Patellar Fracture & Compartment
Syndrome)
Story
Tina watched as the patient winced. "Got into a bar fight. Landed straight on the knee."
Dr. Rao palpated. "Fractured patella. Let’s check compartments — any increased pressure
could cause ischemia."
Lin added, "Pain seems out of proportion, and the leg's getting tense."
Thomas leaned in. "Could be compartment syndrome."
Normal Physiology
The leg contains multiple muscle compartments bound by fascia. Normal perfusion requires
a pressure gradient between capillaries and interstitial space.
Capillary perfusion pressure is typically around 30 mmHg. If compartment pressure rises
above this, tissue ischemia results.
Normal healing from blunt trauma involves local swelling, inflammatory cytokine
recruitment (IL-6, IL-8), and repair.
Pathophysiology
In compartment syndrome, trauma increases fluid or blood in a compartment. Fascia cannot
expand, leading to pressure buildup.
Arterial inflow continues, but venous return is compromised → increased hydrostatic
pressure → worsening edema.
Untreated ischemia causes necrosis of muscle and nerves within hours.
Emergent fasciotomy relieves pressure and restores circulation.
Exam Hint Summary
^^Pain out of proportion to exam is a classic early sign of compartment syndrome^^
^^Pulselessness is a late finding; absence of it does not rule out diagnosis^^
^^Definitive treatment is fasciotomy^^

Chapter 29: Disorders of the Esophagus, Stomach, and Small Intestine
Disorders of the Esophagus
1. Dysphagia
• Pathophysiology:
o Dysphagia is difficulty swallowing, and it can arise from mechanical or motility
dysfunctions in the esophagus or oropharyngeal region.
§ Oropharyngeal dysphagia occurs due to neurological conditions (e.g.,
stroke, Parkinson's disease) that affect the brain's control of swallowing
muscles. This leads to inadequate bolus propulsion and failure to
initiate swallowing.
§ Esophageal dysphagia involves an obstruction or motility issue in the
esophagus. Obstructions like esophageal cancer, strictures, or achalasia
(failure of the LES to relax) impede normal food passage.
§ In achalasia, the loss of peristalsis in the esophageal body and failure of
LES relaxation prevent food from moving to the stomach. The esophagus
dilates above the LES due to retention of food.
^^ This condition is highly associated with neurological or motility disorders, and questions
may focus on the different causes of dysphagia (mechanical vs. motility). Understanding
achalasia's mechanism of action is key.
2. GERD (Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease)
• Pathophysiology:
o GERD occurs when gastric contents (acid, bile, enzymes) reflux into the
esophagus due to a dysfunctional lower esophageal sphincter (LES).
o The LES is usually tonically contracted to prevent reflux, but in GERD, LES
hypotension (weakness) or inappropriate LES relaxation occurs. This allows
acidic stomach contents to reflux into the esophagus, leading to esophageal
mucosal injury.
o Over time, repeated acid exposure causes inflammation (esophagitis), which may
lead to strictures and Barrett's esophagus, where columnar epithelium
replaces the normal squamous epithelium as a compensatory mechanism to
withstand acid injury.
Dr. Modali - HCR240: Module 5 Notes
Arizona College of Nursing
^^ GERD is directly associated with LES dysfunction and esophageal mucosal injury. Focus
on the mechanisms of acid reflux and long-term complications like Barrett's esophagus and
esophageal cancer.
Disorders of the Stomach
3. Hiatal Hernia
• Pathophysiology:
o A hiatal hernia occurs when a portion of the stomach protrudes into the thoracic
cavity through the diaphragmatic hiatus, typically the esophageal hiatus.
o This condition can contribute to GERD, as the herniated portion of the stomach
can cause LES dysfunction, facilitating acid reflux.
o There are two types of hiatal hernia: sliding (more common) where the stomach
slides up into the chest, and paraesophageal where part of the stomach herniates
next to the esophagus but remains in place.
o The hernia can cause esophageal obstruction, dysphagia, and lead to acid
reflux symptoms.
^^ Hiatal hernia may be linked to GERD due to LES dysfunction and esophageal obstruction.
Pay attention to the anatomical differences between sliding and paraesophageal hernias and
their impact on reflux.
4. Pyloric Stenosis
• Pathophysiology:
o Pyloric stenosis is a narrowing of the pyloric sphincter due to hypertrophy of
the pyloric muscle, obstructing the passage of food from the stomach into the
small intestine.
o This condition leads to projectile vomiting in infants, typically after feedings,
because the stomach cannot empty properly into the duodenum.
o The hypertrophic pyloric muscle causes gastric distension and increased gastric
pressure, which may result in dehydration and electrolyte imbalances (e.g.,
hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis from the vomiting).
^^ Pyloric stenosis results from muscle hypertrophy, and it's a common cause of obstruction in
infants. The exam may test electrolyte abnormalities associated with this condition.
5. Acute Gastritis
• Pathophysiology:
o Acute gastritis involves inflammation of the gastric mucosa, often due to injury
from NSAIDs, alcohol, stress, or bacterial infections (e.g., H. pylori).
Dr. Modali - HCR240: Module 5 Notes
Arizona College of Nursing
o The mucosal barrier, which protects the stomach lining from gastric acid,
becomes compromised, leading to mucosal injury and inflammation.
o H. pylori infection disrupts the gastric mucosal integrity through the production
of urease (which neutralizes acid) and the release of cytotoxins that damage
mucosal cells. The resulting inflammation can lead to ulceration if untreated.
^^ Focus on the role of H. pylori in the pathogenesis of gastritis and the relationship between
NSAID use and gastric mucosal damage.
6. Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD)
• Pathophysiology:
o Peptic ulcer disease refers to the formation of ulcers (erosions in the mucosa) in
the stomach or duodenum. It results from an imbalance between gastric acid
production and the defensive mechanisms of the gastric mucosa.
o H. pylori infection is a major cause of PUD, as it produces urease, which
neutralizes stomach acid and weakens the mucosal barrier. This allows acid to
erode the mucosa.
o NSAIDs also inhibit prostaglandins, which are involved in maintaining the
gastric mucosal barrier. This leads to increased gastric acid secretion and
impaired mucosal healing.
o Ulcers are characterized by the loss of epithelial integrity, causing pain, potential
bleeding, and risk of perforation.
^^ PUD is linked to H. pylori infection and NSAID use. Be sure to understand the mechanisms
by which H. pylori damages the mucosal barrier and how NSAIDs contribute to ulcer formation.
Disorders of the Small Intestine
7. Hernia
• Pathophysiology:
o A hernia in the small intestine occurs when part of the intestine protrudes through
a defect in the abdominal wall. This can lead to intestinal obstruction or
strangulation, reducing blood flow to the herniated segment.
o Incarceration refers to a hernia that cannot be reduced (pushed back), and
strangulation involves compromised blood flow, leading to ischemia, necrosis,
and potentially perforation.
^^ Hernias can lead to intestinal obstruction and strangulation. Understanding the difference
between incarceration and strangulation is critical.
8. Gastroenteritis
Dr. Modali - HCR240: Module 5 Notes
Arizona College of Nursing
• Pathophysiology:
o Gastroenteritis is inflammation of the stomach and small intestine, usually
caused by viral (e.g., norovirus, rotavirus) or bacterial infections (e.g.,
Salmonella, E. coli).
o Infection causes mucosal inflammation, leading to diarrhea, vomiting, and
abdominal cramps. The pathogen may produce enterotoxins that increase
intestinal secretion or disrupt the intestinal absorption process.
o In severe cases, infection may lead to dehydration and electrolyte imbalances.
^^ Gastroenteritis results in mucosal inflammation and diarrhea. Focus on the role of
enterotoxins in increasing intestinal secretion.
9. Celiac Disease
• Pathophysiology:
o Celiac disease is an autoimmune disorder triggered by the ingestion of gluten in
genetically predisposed individuals (those with HLA-DQ2 or HLA-DQ8 alleles).
o Gluten triggers an immune response, leading to the production of
autoantibodies (e.g., anti-tissue transglutaminase antibodies). These
antibodies damage the intestinal villi in the small intestine, leading to villous
atrophy and malabsorption of nutrients.
o The inflammatory response involves T-cell mediated immune activation, which
leads to epithelial damage and impaired absorption.
^^ Celiac disease is an autoimmune disorder involving immune-mediated damage to the
small intestine, often leading to malabsorption and nutritional deficiencies.

Case 1: Spicy Spiral (GERD)
Alfie, now a full-fledged nursing student and no longer just a passive observer, clutched his
chest theatrically after lunch in the clinic lounge. "Doc, it feels like I’m breathing fire after
every dinner!"
Dr. Rao raised an eyebrow. "Classic presentation of GERD — gastroesophageal reflux
disease. Let’s teach through this one."
Ashwin chimed in, "Alfie’s diet is like lava — spicy curry, carbonated drinks, and no sleep."
Lin pulled up an esophageal manometry diagram. "Let’s back up and walk through the
physiology."
-- Normal Physiology --
1. The lower esophageal sphincter (LES) acts as a valve between the esophagus and
stomach. It maintains high resting tone to prevent reflux.
2. Esophageal peristalsis clears any incidental refluxate.
3. Saliva contains bicarbonate, which neutralizes acid.
4. Esophageal mucosa is non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium — designed for
abrasion resistance but not acid exposure.
5. Gastric contents include hydrochloric acid, pepsin, and sometimes bile if duodenogastric
reflux occurs.
-- Pathophysiology --
1. In GERD, the LES is weakened or relaxes inappropriately (e.g., after large meals, alcohol,
caffeine, pregnancy).
2. Refluxed acid damages the esophageal lining → inflammation (esophagitis).
3. Chronic exposure can cause metaplasia (Barrett's esophagus), stricture, or ulceration.
4. Symptoms include heartburn, regurgitation, and sometimes chronic cough or laryngitis.
^^Key clues: post-meal burning, worse with lying flat, better with antacids.^^
Dr. Rao added, "Treat first with lifestyle: weight loss, avoiding triggers, elevating the head of
bed. Meds like PPIs are next."
Tina muttered, "Alfie’s going on oatmeal and chamomile for a month."
Case 2: Ashwin’s Abdominal Alarm (Peptic Ulcer Disease)
Ashwin entered clutching his epigastric region. "Feels like a drill in my stomach when I’m
hungry, and it gets better when I eat."
Dr. Rao nodded. "Textbook duodenal ulcer symptoms. Let's go deeper."
-- Normal Physiology --
1. Gastric parietal cells secrete hydrochloric acid (HCl) via H+/K+ ATPase pumps.
2. Chief cells secrete pepsinogen, which is activated to pepsin in acidic pH.
3. The gastric mucosal barrier includes mucus, bicarbonate secretion, tight junctions, and
rapid epithelial turnover.
4. Prostaglandins (especially PGE2) promote mucus and bicarb production and inhibit acid
secretion.
5. In the duodenum, Brunner’s glands and pancreatic secretions neutralize incoming acid.
-- Pathophysiology --
1. Peptic ulcers result from imbalance between aggressive factors (acid, H. pylori, NSAIDs)
and defensive mechanisms (mucus, bicarbonate).
2. H. pylori releases urease, raising local pH and damaging mucosa via inflammation.
3. NSAIDs inhibit COX → ↓ prostaglandins → ↓ mucus and bicarbonate.
4. Duodenal ulcers: pain improves with food. Gastric ulcers: worsens with food.
^^Clues: burning epigastric pain, especially between meals; relief with food or antacids.^^
Thomas whispered, "Let me test for H. pylori using the Oracle breath technique."
Dr. Rao chuckled, "Or just a urea breath test, stool antigen, or biopsy."
Treatment: triple therapy — PPI + clarithromycin + amoxicillin/metronidazole.
Case 3: Hepatic Hiccups (Hepatitis A)
A new patient entered, skin tinged yellow. "I've had fever, nausea, and now my eyes are
yellow."
Dr. Rao asked, "Any recent travel or shellfish?"
The patient nodded. "A week in Cancun. Ate oysters every day."
Dr. Rao turned to the students. "Hepatitis A. Let's unpack it."
-- Normal Physiology --
1. Liver functions: detoxification, protein synthesis, bile production, glycogen storage.
2. Hepatocytes process bilirubin from heme breakdown: unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin is
transported to the liver → conjugated (direct) with glucuronic acid → excreted in bile.
3. Bile aids in fat digestion and carries waste into intestines.
4. Liver also initiates immune response: Kupffer cells (macrophages), APCs, cytokines.
5. ALT (alanine aminotransferase) is cytosolic and specific to hepatocytes. AST (aspartate
aminotransferase) is found in liver, muscle, and other tissues.
6. Hepatocyte injury → leakage of ALT/AST → elevated serum levels.
-- Pathophysiology --
1. Hepatitis A is a single-stranded RNA virus transmitted via fecal-oral route.
2. Virus infects hepatocytes → immune-mediated cell destruction → symptoms.
3. Early symptoms: malaise, fever, nausea, RUQ pain.
4. Later: jaundice, dark urine (↑ conjugated bilirubin), pale stools (↓ stercobilin).
5. Labs: ↑ ALT > AST, ↑ total and direct bilirubin, positive anti-HAV IgM.
^^ALT > AST is classic in viral hepatitis; IgM = acute infection.^^
^^No chronicity with HAV. Supportive care only.^^
Ashwin asked, "Why dark urine?"
Tina answered, "Conjugated bilirubin is water-soluble — gets filtered into urine."
Dr. Rao concluded, "Always ask about travel, food, and vaccination history."
Case 4: Coach Mando’s Midnight Groans (Cholelithiasis and Cholecystitis)
The fluorescent lights of the clinic buzzed faintly as Coach Mando shuffled in, clutching his right
side. “It started last night after my cheat meal,” he groaned. “Burgers, fries, milkshake... the
usual.” Dr. Rao raised an eyebrow. “Pain in the RUQ, radiating to the scapula?” Coach Mando
nodded. Alfie opened his notes. “Could be gallstones—cholelithiasis?” Tina glanced at the
ultrasound requisition pad. “Let’s walk through this.”
Normal Physiology
1. The liver produces bile, which contains bile salts, cholesterol, phospholipids, and bilirubin. Bile
aids in the emulsification and absorption of dietary fats.
2. Bile is stored and concentrated in the gallbladder between meals. Upon ingestion of fatty
foods, cholecystokinin (CCK) is released from the duodenum, stimulating gallbladder contraction

HCR 240 — MODULE 6
THE ENDOCRINE SYSTEM — FULL PHYSIOLOGIC
FOUNDATION
The endocrine system is a long-distance communication system.
Instead of using electrical signals like the nervous system, it uses chemical signals called
hormones that travel through the bloodstream.
The purpose of the endocrine system is to:
Maintain internal balance (homeostasis)
→ regulate metabolism
→ control growth and development
→ coordinate stress responses
→ regulate water balance
→ control reproduction
If this system functions normally, the body remains stable.
If signaling is disrupted, disease develops.
1 — WHAT A HORMONE IS
A hormone is a chemical messenger produced by a gland
→ released into the bloodstream
→ travels to a distant target tissue
→ binds to a specific receptor
→ changes the activity of that cell
The effect of a hormone depends on two things:
The amount of hormone present
AND
The ability of the target tissue to respond
This creates three fundamental patterns:
Hormone deficiency
→ not enough signal reaches the target tissue
Hormone excess
→ too much signal reaches the target tissue
Hormone resistance
→ hormone is present, but the target tissue does not respond
These three patterns explain most endocrine disorders.
2 — HOW HORMONES PRODUCE EFFECTS
Hormones must bind to receptors to work.
There are two major types of hormone signaling:
Water-Soluble Hormones
These hormones cannot pass through the cell membrane.
They bind to receptors on the surface of the cell
→ activate intracellular signaling pathways (second messengers)
→ produce a rapid effect
Examples include peptide hormones such as insulin and ADH ^^
Lipid-Soluble Hormones
These hormones can pass through the cell membrane.
They enter the cell
→ bind to receptors inside the cell (often in the nucleus)
→ directly influence gene expression
→ produce slower but longer-lasting effects
Examples include steroid hormones such as cortisol ^^ and thyroid hormones (T3 and T4) ^^
3 — NEGATIVE FEEDBACK (CORE CONTROL
PRINCIPLE)
Endocrine systems are controlled by feedback loops.
The most important type is negative feedback.
A hormone is released
→ produces its effect
→ that effect signals the body to stop further hormone production
This prevents overactivity and maintains balance.
Without feedback control, hormone levels would become unstable.
4 — THE HYPOTHALAMUS (THE CONTROL CENTER)
The hypothalamus is located in the brain.
It acts as the link between the nervous system and the endocrine system.
The hypothalamus monitors:
Body temperature
Fluid balance
Energy status
Stress levels
Based on this information, it releases regulatory hormones.
These hormones do not act directly on body tissues.
They act on the pituitary gland.
5 — THE PITUITARY GLAND (THE MASTER GLAND)
The pituitary gland is located just below the hypothalamus.
It is divided into two parts:
Anterior pituitary
Posterior pituitary
A. ANTERIOR PITUITARY
The anterior pituitary produces hormones that control other endocrine glands.
These are called tropic hormones.
The sequence is:
Hypothalamus releases a regulatory hormone
→ anterior pituitary releases a tropic hormone
→ tropic hormone stimulates a peripheral endocrine gland
→ that gland produces the final hormone
Example — Thyroid Axis
Hypothalamus releases TRH
→ anterior pituitary releases TSH ^^
→ thyroid gland produces T3 and T4 ^^
→ these hormones act on tissues to increase metabolism
T3 and T4 then inhibit TRH and TSH production
→ this is negative feedback
Example — Adrenal Axis
Hypothalamus releases CRH
→ anterior pituitary releases ACTH
→ adrenal cortex produces cortisol ^^

6 — ANTIDIURETIC HORMONE (ADH) AND WATER
BALANCE
ADH regulates how much water the body retains.
Normal Mechanism
ADH is released
→ travels to the kidneys
→ acts on the collecting ducts
The collecting ducts become more permeable to water
→ water is reabsorbed back into the bloodstream
→ urine becomes concentrated
→ blood volume is maintained
If ADH Is Low
The collecting ducts do not reabsorb water
→ large amounts of dilute urine are produced ^^
→ water is lost from the body
→ blood becomes more concentrated
→ sodium concentration rises
If ADH Is High
Too much water is reabsorbed
→ water accumulates in the body
→ blood becomes diluted
→ sodium concentration falls (hyponatremia) ^^
→ fluid volume increases ^^

9 — THE PANCREAS (GLUCOSE CONTROL)
The pancreas regulates blood glucose levels.
Insulin (Produced by Beta Cells ^^)
When blood glucose rises:
Insulin is released
→ glucose enters cells
→ glucose is stored as glycogen
→ blood glucose decreases
Glucagon (Produced by Alpha Cells)
When blood glucose falls:
Glucagon is released
→ glycogen is broken down
→ glucose enters bloodstream
→ blood glucose increases
Balance
Insulin lowers glucose
Glucagon raises glucose
Together they maintain stable blood sugar levels.

15 — ISCHEMIC STROKE (LOSS OF BLOOD FLOW)
Etiology
An ischemic stroke occurs when blood flow to part of the brain is reduced or blocked.
Common mechanisms include:
Atherosclerosis ^^
→ gradual buildup of lipid plaques in arterial walls
→ narrowing of vessel lumen
→ reduced blood flow
Carotid stenosis ^^
→ narrowing of carotid arteries
→ decreased cerebral perfusion
Atrial fibrillation ^^
→ irregular atrial contraction
→ blood stasis in atria
→ thrombus formation
→ embolus travels to cerebral circulation
Pathophysiology (FULL STEPWISE CHAIN)
Reduction in blood flow
→ decreased oxygen delivery to neurons
Oxygen is required for oxidative phosphorylation
→ oxidative phosphorylation produces ATP
Decreased oxygen
→ decreased ATP production
ATP is required for Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase pump
→ this pump maintains ion gradients
When ATP decreases
→ Na⁺/K⁺ pump fails
Na⁺ accumulates inside neuron
→ osmotic gradient develops
→ water enters neuron
Water influx
→ cellular swelling (cytotoxic edema)
Swollen neurons become dysfunctional
At the same time:
Ischemia triggers glutamate release ^^
→ glutamate accumulates in synapse
Glutamate activates NMDA receptors
→ excessive Ca²⁺ enters neuron
Calcium overload
→ activates destructive enzymes
→ damages cell structures
→ leads to cell death
Structural Consequence
Area with no blood flow
→ core infarct (irreversible damage)
Surrounding area
→ ischemic penumbra ^^
Penumbra has reduced blood flow
→ partial ATP production maintained
→ cells are injured but not dead
Functional Consequence
Loss of function depends on brain region affected:
Motor cortex → weakness
Language centers → speech difficulty
Brainstem → life-threatening dysfunction
Clinical Presentation
Hemiparesis ^^ (partial weakness)
Hemiparalysis ^^ (complete paralysis)
Facial droop
Slurred speech
Diagnostic Logic
Sudden onset neurologic deficit
→ suggests acute interruption of blood flow
