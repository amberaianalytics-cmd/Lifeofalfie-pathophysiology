1. Disorders of the Immune System
Immune disorders develop when immune regulation becomes abnormal.
Major immune disease categories include:
•	autoimmunity
•	hypersensitivity
•	immunodeficiency ^^
The immune system must distinguish:
•	self
from
•	non-self
Failure of immune regulation
→ autoreactive lymphocytes survive
→ inappropriate immune activation develops
→ inflammation and tissue injury occur
 
1.1 Immune Tolerance
Immune tolerance prevents the immune system from attacking normal self-tissue.
Developing lymphocytes generate random antigen receptors.
Random receptor generation is necessary because the immune system must recognize many different pathogens.
However:
Random receptor generation
→ some lymphocytes recognize self-antigens
→ potentially dangerous autoreactive lymphocytes form
Tolerance mechanisms therefore eliminate or suppress self-reactive lymphocytes.
Tolerance occurs through:
•	central tolerance
•	peripheral tolerance
 
1.2 Central Tolerance
Central tolerance occurs during lymphocyte development.
For T cells, this occurs primarily within the thymus.
Developing T cells undergo:
•	positive selection
•	negative selection
 
1.2A Positive Selection
Positive selection tests whether immature T cells can recognize MHC molecules.
Mechanism:
Immature T cell enters thymus
→ thymic epithelial cells display self-MHC
→ T-cell receptor attempts MHC binding
No MHC recognition
→ apoptosis occurs
Reason:
T cells must recognize antigen presented on MHC molecules.
No MHC recognition
→ T cell cannot function
→ nonfunctional T cell removed
Weak or moderate MHC recognition
→ T cell survives
→ positive selection successful
Positive selection therefore preserves functional T cells.
 
1.2B Negative Selection
Negative selection tests whether T cells react strongly against self-antigens.
Mechanism:
Surviving T cells encounter self-antigens in thymus
→ strong self-antigen binding occurs
→ apoptosis triggered
→ autoreactive T cell destroyed
Strong self-recognition is dangerous because the T cell may later attack normal tissue.
Negative selection therefore removes dangerous autoreactive lymphocytes.
Failure of negative selection
→ autoreactive T cells survive
→ autoimmune disease risk increases
 
1.3 Peripheral Tolerance
Some autoreactive lymphocytes escape central tolerance.
Peripheral tolerance suppresses these cells outside the thymus and bone marrow.
Mechanisms include:
•	anergy
•	regulatory T cells
•	apoptosis
 
1.3A Anergy
Anergy means functional inactivation.
T-cell activation normally requires:
•	antigen recognition
•	co-stimulation
If antigen recognition occurs without proper co-stimulation:
T cell recognizes antigen
→ incomplete activation occurs
→ T cell becomes inactive
→ tissue attack prevented
 
1.3B Regulatory T Cells
Regulatory T cells suppress excessive immune activation.
Mechanism:
Regulatory T cell recognizes excessive immune response
→ suppressive cytokines released
→ autoreactive lymphocyte activity decreases
→ tissue injury limited
 
1.4 Autoimmunity ^^
Autoimmunity develops when tolerance mechanisms fail. ^^
Mechanism:
Failure of central or peripheral tolerance
→ autoreactive lymphocytes survive
→ self-antigens recognized as dangerous
→ cytokine release and antibody production occur
→ chronic inflammation develops
→ tissue injury occurs
Autoimmune disease therefore represents loss of immune regulation rather than “strong immunity.”
 
1.5 Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) ^^
SLE is a systemic autoimmune disease associated with immune-complex deposition. ^^
Mechanism:
Failure of immune tolerance
→ autoreactive B cells survive
→ autoantibodies form against nuclear antigens
→ antigen-antibody immune complexes form
→ immune complexes circulate
→ immune complexes deposit in tissues
→ complement activated
→ neutrophils recruited
→ inflammatory tissue injury develops
Important autoantibodies include:
•	ANA ^^
•	anti-dsDNA ^^
 
1.5A SLE and Joint Pain ^^
Immune complexes deposit in synovial tissue.
Immune-complex deposition
→ complement activation
→ neutrophil recruitment
→ inflammatory cytokine release
→ synovial inflammation develops
→ joint stiffness and pain occur ^^
 
1.5B SLE and Butterfly Rash ^^
Immune complexes deposit within dermal blood vessels.
Immune-complex deposition in skin
→ complement activation
→ vascular inflammation develops
→ tissue injury occurs
→ malar butterfly rash develops across cheeks and nose ^^
Photosensitivity worsens inflammation because UV injury increases cellular damage and antigen exposure.
 
1.5C SLE and Hypertension ^^
Immune complexes deposit in glomeruli.
Immune-complex deposition in glomeruli
→ glomerulonephritis develops
→ glomerular filtration decreases
→ sodium and water retention increase
→ intravascular volume increases
→ hypertension develops ^^
Proteinuria develops because inflammatory glomerular injury increases protein leakage through the filtration barrier.
 
1.5D SLE Clinical Findings ^^
Common manifestations include:
•	fatigue
•	fever ^^
•	painful or stiff joints ^^
•	butterfly rash ^^
•	photosensitivity
•	renal dysfunction
•	hypertension from renal involvement ^^
•	positive ANA ^^
•	positive anti-dsDNA ^^
SLE is classified as Type III hypersensitivity because tissue injury is caused by immune-complex deposition. ^^
 
1.6 Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA) ^^
Rheumatoid arthritis is chronic autoimmune inflammatory joint disease. ^^
Mechanism:
Failure of immune tolerance
→ autoreactive lymphocytes target synovial tissue
→ cytokine release develops
→ chronic synovial inflammation occurs
→ pannus forms
→ cartilage destruction develops
→ bone erosion develops
→ joint deformity occurs
Important cytokines include:
•	TNF-alpha
•	IL-1
•	IL-6
Pannus is inflammatory granulation tissue growing across the joint surface.
Pannus formation
→ cartilage erosion
→ narrowing of joint space
→ pain and stiffness worsen
→ deformity develops
RA typically affects joints symmetrically.
Common findings include:
•	morning stiffness
•	swollen joints
•	painful joints
•	decreased range of motion
•	chronic hand deformities
 
1.7 Hypersensitivity Reactions ^^
Hypersensitivity reactions are exaggerated or misdirected immune responses causing tissue injury. ^^
The four major hypersensitivity reactions are:
•	Type I hypersensitivity ^^
•	Type II hypersensitivity ^^
•	Type III hypersensitivity ^^
•	Type IV hypersensitivity ^^
 
1.8 Type I Hypersensitivity ^^
Type I hypersensitivity is immediate hypersensitivity mediated by IgE and mast cells. ^^
Mechanism:
Initial allergen exposure
→ helper T-cell activation occurs
→ B-cell class switching to IgE occurs
→ IgE binds mast-cell Fc receptors
→ sensitization develops
Re-exposure to same allergen
→ allergen cross-links mast-cell-bound IgE
→ mast-cell degranulation occurs
→ histamine released
→ immediate allergic symptoms develop
Histamine causes:
•	vasodilation
•	increased vascular permeability
•	itching
•	mucus production
•	bronchoconstriction
Important mediators and cells include:
•	IgE ^^
•	mast cells ^^
 
1.8A Allergic Rhinitis and Hay Fever ^^
Allergen exposure
→ mast-cell activation
→ histamine release
→ vascular permeability increases
→ mucus secretion increases
→ nasal inflammation develops ^^
Common findings include:
•	rhinorrhea
•	sneezing
•	itching
•	congestion
Hay fever ^^
and
allergic rhinitis ^^
are Type I hypersensitivity reactions.
 
1.8B Urticaria ^^
Histamine release within superficial dermis
→ vascular permeability increases
→ localized edema develops
→ raised pruritic wheals form
Urticaria ^^
is commonly called hives.
 
1.8C Anaphylaxis ^^
Anaphylaxis is severe systemic Type I hypersensitivity. ^^
Mechanism:
Systemic allergen exposure
→ widespread mast-cell degranulation
→ massive histamine release
→ systemic vasodilation develops
→ vascular permeability increases
→ hypotension develops
→ bronchoconstriction develops
→ airway edema develops
→ respiratory compromise may occur
Epinephrine treatment:
Epinephrine
→ vasoconstriction increases blood pressure
→ bronchodilation improves airflow
 
1.9 Type II Hypersensitivity ^^
Type II hypersensitivity occurs when antibodies bind directly to cell-surface antigens. ^^
Primary antibodies involved:
•	IgG
•	IgM
Mechanism:
Antibody binds cell-surface antigen
→ complement activated
→ inflammatory destruction occurs
→ target cells damaged
 
1.9A Transfusion Reaction ^^
Incompatible blood transfusion
→ recipient antibodies bind donor RBC antigens
→ complement activated
→ RBC hemolysis develops
→ inflammatory reaction occurs ^^
Hemolysis releases hemoglobin into circulation.
Possible findings include:
•	fever
•	chills
•	hypotension
•	hemoglobinuria
•	renal injury
 
1.9B Hemolytic Disease of the Newborn ^^
Rh-negative mother exposed to Rh-positive fetal blood
→ maternal anti-Rh antibodies form
→ maternal IgG crosses placenta during later pregnancy
→ fetal RBC destruction develops
→ fetal hemolytic anemia occurs ^^
RBC destruction
→ bilirubin production increases
→ jaundice develops
Severe bilirubin accumulation may cause kernicterus.
Phototherapy ^^
helps reduce bilirubin accumulation.
 
1.10 Type III Hypersensitivity ^^
Type III hypersensitivity occurs when immune complexes deposit within tissues. ^^
Mechanism:
Antigen-antibody complexes form
→ immune complexes circulate
→ immune complexes deposit in tissues
→ complement activated
→ neutrophils recruited
→ inflammatory tissue injury develops
Common deposition sites include:
•	kidneys
•	joints
•	skin
•	blood vessels
SLE ^^
is a classic Type III hypersensitivity disease.
 
1.11 Type IV Hypersensitivity ^^
Type IV hypersensitivity is delayed T-cell-mediated hypersensitivity. ^^
Antibodies are not the primary mechanism.
Mechanism:
Antigen exposure
→ sensitized T cells form
→ re-exposure activates memory T cells
→ cytokine release occurs
→ macrophages activated
→ delayed inflammatory injury develops
Type IV reactions usually develop over 24–72 hours.
Examples include:
•	poison ivy ^^
•	contact dermatitis ^^
•	tuberculosis skin test ^^
 
1.12 Immunodeficiency ^^
Immunodeficiency occurs when immune defense mechanisms are impaired. ^^
Mechanism:
Defective immune cells
or
Defective antibodies
or
Defective immune signaling
→ poor pathogen clearance develops
→ recurrent infections occur
Immunodeficiency disorders may affect:
•	T cells
•	B cells
•	antibodies
•	combined immunity
 
1.13 Severe Combined Immunodeficiency Disease (SCID) ^^
SCID is severe combined impairment of both cellular and humoral immunity. ^^
Mechanism:
Genetic defect impairs lymphocyte development
→ T-cell maturation impaired
→ helper T-cell function decreases
→ B-cell activation impaired
→ antibody responses weaken
→ severe adaptive immune dysfunction develops
Impaired T-cell function
→ viral and fungal defense weakened
Impaired B-cell activation
→ bacterial defense weakened
Common findings include:
•	recurrent respiratory infections ^^
•	recurrent pneumonia ^^
•	recurrent bronchitis ^^
•	opportunistic infections
•	failure to thrive
•	frequent antibiotic treatment ^^
 
1.14 Selective IgA Deficiency ^^
Selective IgA deficiency is decreased IgA production. ^^
IgA normally protects mucosal surfaces.
Reduced IgA production
→ decreased mucosal immunity
→ pathogens attach more easily to respiratory and GI mucosa
→ recurrent mucosal infections develop
Common findings include:
•	recurrent sinus infections
•	recurrent respiratory infections ^^
•	recurrent GI infections
•	chronic mucosal infections
Selective IgA deficiency ^^
is one of the most common primary immunodeficiency disorders.
 
1.15 Hypogammaglobulinemia ^^
Hypogammaglobulinemia involves decreased immunoglobulin production. ^^
Mechanism:
Reduced antibody production
→ impaired opsonization and neutralization
→ recurrent bacterial infections develop
Common findings include:
•	recurrent respiratory infections
•	chronic sinus infections
•	recurrent pneumonia
 
1.16 Chronic Mucocutaneous Candidiasis ^^
Chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis involves persistent Candida infections affecting skin and mucosal surfaces. ^^
Mechanism:
T-cell dysfunction develops
→ impaired fungal immunity occurs
→ chronic Candida overgrowth develops
Common findings include:
•	oral thrush
•	chronic fungal skin infections
•	mucosal Candida infections
 
1.17 DiGeorge Syndrome ^^
DiGeorge syndrome is associated with abnormal thymic development. ^^
Mechanism:
22q11 deletion
→ thymic hypoplasia develops
→ T-cell maturation impaired
→ cellular immunity weakened
Reduced T-cell function
→ impaired viral defense
→ impaired fungal defense
Associated findings may include:
•	recurrent infections
•	congenital heart defects
•	hypocalcemia
 
1.18 HIV and AIDS ^^
HIV infects CD4 helper T cells. ^^
Mechanism:
HIV enters bloodstream
→ virus binds CD4 receptors
→ viral entry into helper T cells occurs
→ reverse transcriptase converts viral RNA into DNA
→ viral DNA integrates into host genome
→ viral replication develops
→ CD4 helper T-cell destruction occurs
Loss of helper T cells
→ impaired immune coordination develops
→ macrophage activation weakens
→ cytotoxic T-cell activation weakens
→ B-cell activation weakens
→ opportunistic infections increase
AIDS ^^
develops when severe CD4 depletion causes profound immunodeficiency.

 
2. Disorders of White Blood Cells and Hematologic Malignancy
White blood cells normally defend the body against infection, abnormal cells, and foreign material.
Major leukocyte groups include:
•	neutrophils
•	lymphocytes
•	monocytes/macrophages
•	eosinophils
•	basophils
Normal hematopoiesis occurs in bone marrow.
Hematopoietic stem cell
→ myeloid stem cells and lymphoid stem cells form
→ mature blood cells produced
→ functional immune defense maintained
Disorders develop when:
•	leukocyte production becomes malignant
•	leukocyte maturation fails
•	leukocyte numbers become abnormal
•	leukocyte function becomes impaired
 
2.1 Leukocytosis ^^
Leukocytosis means elevated white blood cell count. ^^
Mechanism:
Infection or inflammation
→ cytokines stimulate bone marrow
→ leukocyte production increases
→ circulating WBC count rises
Different leukocyte elevations suggest different disease processes.
 
2.1A Neutrophilia ^^
Neutrophils are major phagocytic cells involved in acute bacterial inflammation.
Bacterial infection
→ macrophage cytokine release develops
→ bone marrow stimulation occurs
→ neutrophil production increases
→ neutrophilia develops ^^
Neutrophilia commonly suggests:
•	bacterial infection ^^
•	acute inflammation
•	tissue necrosis
•	stress response
 
2.1B Lymphocytosis ^^
Lymphocytes are central to adaptive immunity.
Viral infection
→ lymphocyte activation develops
→ lymphocyte proliferation increases
→ lymphocytosis develops ^^
Lymphocytosis commonly suggests:
•	viral infection ^^
•	infectious mononucleosis ^^
•	chronic immune stimulation
 
2.1C Eosinophilia ^^
Eosinophils participate in allergic disease and parasitic defense.
Parasitic infection or allergic stimulation
→ IL-5 production increases
→ eosinophil production increases
→ eosinophilia develops ^^
Eosinophilia commonly suggests:
•	parasitic infection ^^
•	allergic disease ^^
•	asthma
•	hypersensitivity reactions
 
2.2 Infectious Mononucleosis ^^
Infectious mononucleosis is commonly caused by Epstein-Barr virus. ^^
Mechanism:
EBV infects B lymphocytes
→ infected B cells proliferate
→ cytotoxic T-cell response develops
→ lymphoid tissue enlarges
→ systemic inflammatory symptoms develop
Common findings include:
•	fever
•	sore throat
•	lymphadenopathy ^^
•	fatigue ^^
•	atypical lymphocytes ^^
Atypical lymphocytes are reactive cytotoxic T cells responding to infected B cells.
 
2.3 Leukopenia ^^
Leukopenia means decreased white blood cell count. ^^
Mechanism:
Bone marrow suppression
or
Immune destruction
or
Viral suppression
→ leukocyte numbers decrease
→ infection risk increases
Causes may include:
•	chemotherapy ^^
•	radiation exposure
•	severe viral infection
•	bone marrow failure
Reduced leukocyte count
→ impaired pathogen defense
→ opportunistic infection risk increases
 
2.4 Leukemia ^^
Leukemia is malignant proliferation of white blood cell precursors within bone marrow. ^^
Mechanism:
DNA mutation develops in hematopoietic precursor cell
→ malignant clone forms
→ uncontrolled leukocyte proliferation develops
→ abnormal leukocytes accumulate in marrow and blood
Bone marrow overcrowding develops.
Malignant leukocyte expansion
→ normal hematopoiesis suppressed
→ anemia develops
→ thrombocytopenia develops
→ infection risk increases
Leukemia therefore often produces:
•	fatigue from anemia
•	bleeding from thrombocytopenia
•	infection from dysfunctional leukocytes
 
2.4A Acute Leukemia ^^
Acute leukemia involves immature blast cells. ^^
Mechanism:
Mutation in immature precursor cell
→ blast-cell proliferation develops
→ maturation failure occurs
→ immature nonfunctional leukocytes accumulate rapidly
Because blast cells fail to mature:
Large numbers of dysfunctional cells accumulate
→ normal marrow function collapses rapidly
→ severe symptoms develop quickly
Common findings include:
•	fatigue ^^
•	bleeding ^^
•	recurrent infections ^^
•	bone pain
•	blast cells in blood or marrow ^^
Acute leukemia progresses rapidly.
 
2.4B Chronic Leukemia ^^
Chronic leukemia involves more mature malignant leukocytes. ^^
Mechanism:
Malignant transformation occurs in more differentiated leukocyte lineage
→ malignant cells continue partial maturation
→ slower accumulation develops
Because cells are somewhat more mature:
Disease progression is often slower initially.
Patients may initially have:
•	leukocytosis ^^
•	fatigue
•	splenomegaly
•	weight loss
 
2.5 Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL) ^^
ALL is malignant proliferation of lymphoblasts. ^^
Mechanism:
Mutation develops in lymphoid precursor cell
→ lymphoblast proliferation develops
→ marrow overcrowding occurs
→ normal blood-cell production suppressed
ALL commonly occurs in children. ^^
Bone marrow failure develops because malignant blasts replace normal marrow tissue.
Consequences include:
•	anemia
•	thrombocytopenia
•	infection risk
•	fatigue ^^
•	bleeding ^^
 
2.6 Acute Myelogenous Leukemia (AML) ^^
AML is malignant proliferation of myeloid precursor cells. ^^
Mechanism:
Mutation develops in myeloid precursor
→ myeloblast proliferation develops
→ marrow failure develops
→ dysfunctional leukocytes accumulate
Auer rods may be seen in myeloblasts. ^^
AML commonly occurs in adults. ^^
 
2.7 Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia (CLL) ^^
CLL involves malignant proliferation of mature-appearing B lymphocytes. ^^
Mechanism:
Malignant B-cell clone develops
→ slow lymphocyte accumulation occurs
→ abnormal lymphocytes circulate in blood and marrow
Common findings include:
•	lymphocytosis ^^
•	lymphadenopathy ^^
•	splenomegaly
•	fatigue
Smudge cells may appear on peripheral smear. ^^
 
2.8 Chronic Myelogenous Leukemia (CML) ^^
CML is associated with BCR-ABL fusion gene formation. ^^
Mechanism:
Chromosomal translocation develops
→ Philadelphia chromosome forms ^^
→ BCR-ABL tyrosine kinase produced
→ uncontrolled myeloid proliferation develops
BCR-ABL continuously stimulates growth signaling.
Consequences:
Excessive granulocyte production
→ marked leukocytosis develops
→ splenomegaly develops
→ hypermetabolic symptoms develop
Common findings include:
•	fatigue
•	weight loss
•	night sweats
•	leukocytosis ^^
•	splenomegaly
Tyrosine kinase inhibitors target abnormal BCR-ABL signaling.
 
2.9 Lymphoma ^^
Lymphoma is malignant proliferation of lymphoid tissue. ^^
Unlike leukemia, lymphoma primarily forms solid masses in lymphoid tissue.
Mechanism:
Mutation develops in lymphocyte
→ malignant lymphocyte clone proliferates
→ lymph-node architecture replaced
→ lymphatic masses enlarge
Common findings include:
•	lymphadenopathy ^^
•	fever
•	night sweats ^^
•	weight loss ^^
These are called “B symptoms.”
 
2.9A Hodgkin Lymphoma ^^
Hodgkin lymphoma is associated with Reed-Sternberg cells. ^^
Mechanism:
Malignant B-cell transformation develops
→ abnormal giant multinucleated cells form
→ inflammatory background develops
→ progressive lymph-node enlargement occurs
Reed-Sternberg cells ^^
are classically associated with Hodgkin lymphoma.
Lymph-node spread is often orderly and contiguous.
 
2.9B Non-Hodgkin Lymphoma ^^
Non-Hodgkin lymphoma includes multiple malignant lymphoid neoplasms without Reed-Sternberg cells. ^^
Spread is often less predictable than Hodgkin lymphoma.
Mechanism:
Malignant lymphocyte clone develops
→ lymphoid tissue proliferation occurs
→ extranodal spread may occur
 
2.10 Multiple Myeloma ^^
Multiple myeloma is malignant proliferation of plasma cells. ^^
Plasma cells normally produce antibodies.
Mechanism:
Malignant plasma-cell clone develops
→ monoclonal immunoglobulin production increases
→ plasma cells accumulate in marrow
→ bone destruction develops
Malignant plasma cells stimulate osteoclast activity.
Osteoclast activation
→ bone resorption increases
→ lytic bone lesions develop
→ fractures and bone pain occur ^^
Bone destruction releases calcium.
Bone resorption
→ hypercalcemia develops ^^
Abnormal immunoglobulins may damage kidneys.
Monoclonal protein accumulation
→ renal injury develops ^^
Common findings include:
•	bone pain ^^
•	lytic lesions ^^
•	hypercalcemia ^^
•	renal dysfunction ^^
•	recurrent infections
•	monoclonal spike on electrophoresis ^^
 
3. Disorders of Red Blood Cells and Oxygen Transport
Red blood cells transport oxygen to tissues.
Normal oxygen delivery requires:
•	adequate RBC production
•	adequate hemoglobin
•	normal iron availability
•	normal bone marrow function
•	normal RBC survival
•	adequate circulation
Hemoglobin inside RBCs binds oxygen in the lungs and delivers oxygen to tissues.
Reduced RBC function
or
Reduced hemoglobin
→ tissue oxygen delivery decreases
→ cellular hypoxia develops
→ fatigue and organ dysfunction occur
 
3.1 Erythropoiesis
Red blood cells are produced in bone marrow.
Mechanism:
Renal oxygen sensing detects hypoxia
→ erythropoietin release increases
→ bone marrow stimulated
→ erythrocyte production increases
Erythropoietin is primarily produced by the kidneys.
Chronic kidney disease
→ decreased erythropoietin production
→ decreased RBC production
→ anemia develops ^^
 
3.2 Anemia ^^
Anemia means reduced oxygen-carrying capacity of blood. ^^
Anemia may result from:
•	blood loss
•	decreased RBC production
•	increased RBC destruction
Reduced oxygen delivery
→ tissue hypoxia develops
→ compensatory physiologic responses occur
Compensatory responses include:
•	tachycardia
•	increased respiratory rate
•	fatigue
•	weakness
Cells shift toward anaerobic metabolism when oxygen delivery decreases.
Reduced oxygen delivery
→ decreased oxidative phosphorylation
→ decreased ATP production
→ increased anaerobic glycolysis
→ lactate production increases
 
3.3 Iron Metabolism
Iron is necessary for hemoglobin synthesis.
Normal mechanism:
Dietary iron absorbed in small intestine
→ iron transported in blood by transferrin
→ iron stored as ferritin
→ iron incorporated into hemoglobin during erythropoiesis
Without adequate iron:
Hemoglobin synthesis decreases
→ RBC oxygen-carrying capacity decreases
→ microcytic hypochromic anemia develops
 
3.4 Iron Deficiency Anemia ^^
Iron deficiency anemia is the most common anemia worldwide. ^^
Mechanism:
Chronic blood loss
or
Poor iron intake
or
Malabsorption
→ iron stores decrease
→ hemoglobin synthesis impaired
→ smaller RBCs produced
→ oxygen delivery decreases
Because hemoglobin decreases:
RBCs become:
•	microcytic ^^
•	hypochromic ^^
Microcytic means small RBCs.
Hypochromic means decreased hemoglobin concentration.
Common causes include:
•	chronic GI bleeding ^^
•	menstruation
•	poor dietary intake
•	peptic ulcer disease
•	colon cancer ^^
Reduced oxygen delivery produces:
•	fatigue ^^
•	weakness
•	pallor ^^
•	dyspnea
•	tachycardia
 
3.5 Ferritin and Transferrin
Ferritin stores iron.
Iron deficiency
→ ferritin decreases ^^
Transferrin transports iron through blood.
Iron deficiency
→ transferrin production increases
→ total iron-binding capacity increases ^^
The body increases transferrin in an attempt to capture more available iron.
 
3.6 Megaloblastic Anemia ^^
Megaloblastic anemia develops when DNA synthesis becomes impaired. ^^
Major causes include:
•	vitamin B12 deficiency ^^
•	folate deficiency ^^
DNA synthesis impairment
→ nuclear maturation slows
→ RBC precursor division impaired
→ abnormally large RBCs develop
RBCs become:
•	macrocytic ^^
•	megaloblastic ^^
Because rapidly dividing marrow cells cannot divide normally:
Ineffective erythropoiesis develops
→ anemia occurs
 
3.6A Vitamin B12 Deficiency ^^
Vitamin B12 is necessary for DNA synthesis and neurologic function. ^^
Normal mechanism:
Dietary B12 binds intrinsic factor in stomach
→ B12 absorbed in terminal ileum
→ normal DNA synthesis maintained
Pernicious anemia develops when intrinsic factor decreases.
Autoimmune destruction of parietal cells
→ intrinsic factor decreases
→ B12 absorption impaired
→ megaloblastic anemia develops ^^
Neurologic injury may also occur.
B12 deficiency
→ abnormal myelin maintenance
→ neurologic dysfunction develops ^^
Possible findings include:
•	paresthesias ^^
•	gait abnormalities
•	neuropathy
•	cognitive changes
 
3.6B Folate Deficiency ^^
Folate is also necessary for DNA synthesis. ^^
Folate deficiency
→ impaired DNA synthesis
→ macrocytic anemia develops ^^
Unlike B12 deficiency:
Folate deficiency usually does not cause severe neurologic deficits.
Causes include:
•	malnutrition
•	alcoholism ^^
•	malabsorption
•	pregnancy
Alcoholism contributes because poor nutrition and impaired absorption decrease folate availability.
 
3.7 Hemolytic Anemia ^^
Hemolytic anemia develops when RBC destruction exceeds RBC production. ^^
Mechanism:
RBC destruction increases
→ hemoglobin released
→ bilirubin production increases
→ jaundice may develop
→ oxygen-carrying capacity decreases
Bone marrow attempts compensation.
Hemolysis
→ erythropoietin increases
→ reticulocyte production increases
 
3.7A Bilirubin Metabolism and Jaundice ^^
Hemoglobin breakdown releases heme.
Heme metabolism
→ unconjugated bilirubin produced
→ bilirubin transported to liver
→ conjugation occurs
→ bilirubin excreted into bile
Excessive hemolysis
→ bilirubin production increases
→ jaundice develops ^^
Bilirubin accumulation causes yellow discoloration of:
•	skin
•	sclera
•	mucous membranes
 
3.8 Sickle Cell Disease ^^
Sickle cell disease is caused by abnormal hemoglobin S formation. ^^
Mechanism:
Beta-globin mutation develops
→ hemoglobin S produced
→ low oxygen conditions trigger hemoglobin polymerization
→ RBC deformation develops
→ sickled RBCs form
Sickled RBCs become rigid.
Rigid RBCs
→ microvascular obstruction develops
→ tissue ischemia develops
→ severe pain occurs ^^
Microvascular obstruction
→ infarction risk increases
→ organ injury develops
Repeated sickling also damages RBC membranes.
Membrane damage
→ hemolysis develops
→ chronic anemia develops ^^
Common findings include:
•	pain crises ^^
•	anemia ^^
•	jaundice
•	splenic dysfunction
•	infection risk increases
Splenic injury develops because repeated infarction damages splenic tissue.
 
3.9 Thalassemia ^^
Thalassemia involves decreased globin-chain production. ^^
Mechanism:
Reduced globin synthesis
→ defective hemoglobin formation
→ ineffective erythropoiesis develops
→ microcytic anemia develops ^^
Because hemoglobin synthesis decreases:
RBCs become small and fragile.
Severe forms may produce:
•	marrow expansion
•	skeletal deformities
•	splenomegaly
 
3.10 Aplastic Anemia ^^
Aplastic anemia is bone marrow failure. ^^
Mechanism:
Stem-cell destruction develops
→ marrow cellularity decreases
→ RBC production decreases
→ WBC production decreases
→ platelet production decreases
Pancytopenia develops.
Pancytopenia means reduction of:
•	RBCs
•	WBCs
•	platelets
Consequences include:
•	fatigue from anemia ^^
•	infection risk from leukopenia ^^
•	bleeding risk from thrombocytopenia ^^
Causes may include:
•	radiation ^^
•	chemotherapy ^^
•	toxins
•	autoimmune marrow injury
 
3.11 Polycythemia ^^
Polycythemia means increased RBC concentration. ^^
Mechanism:
Excess erythrocyte production develops
→ blood viscosity increases
→ circulation slows
→ thrombosis risk increases ^^
Primary polycythemia may result from marrow mutation.
Secondary polycythemia may result from chronic hypoxia.
Chronic hypoxia
→ erythropoietin increases
→ RBC production increases
Common causes include:
•	COPD ^^
•	high altitude ^^
•	chronic hypoxemia
Increased blood viscosity may produce:
•	headache
•	hypertension
•	thrombosis
•	stroke risk
 
3.12 Blood Loss and Hemorrhagic Anemia ^^
Acute hemorrhage
→ circulating blood volume decreases
→ oxygen delivery decreases
→ hypovolemic compensation develops
Compensatory responses include:
•	tachycardia
•	vasoconstriction
•	increased sympathetic activation
Severe hemorrhage
→ tissue perfusion decreases
→ shock may develop ^^
Chronic blood loss
→ iron depletion develops
→ iron deficiency anemia occurs ^^

4. Disorders of Platelets, Coagulation, and Thrombosis
Normal hemostasis prevents excessive bleeding while maintaining blood flow through vessels.
Hemostasis occurs through two major processes:
•	primary hemostasis
•	secondary hemostasis
Primary hemostasis forms the platelet plug.
Secondary hemostasis stabilizes the platelet plug using fibrin.
Disorders develop when:
•	platelets become abnormal
•	coagulation factors become deficient
•	inappropriate clotting develops
•	excessive bleeding develops
 
4.1 Primary Hemostasis
Primary hemostasis forms the initial platelet plug.
Mechanism:
Endothelial injury
→ subendothelial collagen exposed
→ von Willebrand factor binds collagen
→ platelet adhesion occurs
→ platelet activation occurs
→ platelet aggregation occurs
→ temporary platelet plug forms
Activated platelets release:
•	ADP
•	thromboxane A2
•	calcium
ADP recruits additional platelets.
Thromboxane A2 promotes platelet activation and vasoconstriction.
GpIIb/IIIa receptors bind fibrinogen between platelets.
Fibrinogen bridging
→ platelet aggregation develops
→ platelet plug strengthened
 
4.2 Secondary Hemostasis
Secondary hemostasis stabilizes the platelet plug using fibrin.
The coagulation cascade contains:
•	intrinsic pathway
•	extrinsic pathway
•	common pathway
Both pathways activate Factor X.
Factor X activation
→ thrombin generated
→ fibrinogen converted into fibrin
→ stable clot forms
 
4.3 Intrinsic Pathway ^^
The intrinsic pathway begins when blood contacts exposed collagen. ^^
Mechanism:
Collagen exposure
→ Factor XII activated
→ Factor XI activated
→ Factor IX activated
→ Factor VIII activated
→ Factor X activated
PTT measures intrinsic pathway function. ^^
Intrinsic pathway disorders commonly prolong PTT. ^^
 
4.4 Extrinsic Pathway ^^
The extrinsic pathway begins with tissue factor exposure. ^^
Mechanism:
Tissue injury
→ tissue factor released
→ Factor VII activated
→ Factor X activated
PT measures extrinsic pathway function. ^^
Extrinsic pathway disorders commonly prolong PT. ^^
 
4.5 Common Pathway ^^
Both intrinsic and extrinsic pathways converge at Factor X. ^^
Mechanism:
Factor X activation
→ prothrombin converted into thrombin
→ fibrinogen converted into fibrin
→ fibrin cross-linking occurs
→ stable clot develops
Thrombin is central because it converts soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin strands.
 
4.6 Platelet Disorders
Platelet disorders impair primary hemostasis.
Platelet dysfunction
or
Low platelet count
→ impaired platelet-plug formation develops
→ mucosal and superficial bleeding increase
Platelet disorders commonly produce:
•	petechiae ^^
•	purpura ^^
•	mucosal bleeding ^^
•	epistaxis
•	gingival bleeding
Petechiae are small pinpoint hemorrhages caused by capillary bleeding.
Purpura are larger hemorrhagic skin lesions.
 
4.7 Thrombocytopenia ^^
Thrombocytopenia means decreased platelet count. ^^
Mechanism:
Decreased platelet production
or
Increased platelet destruction
or
Platelet consumption
→ platelet numbers decrease
→ bleeding risk increases
Common causes include:
•	bone marrow failure ^^
•	chemotherapy ^^
•	immune destruction
•	DIC ^^
•	hypersplenism
Low platelet count
→ impaired primary hemostasis
→ petechiae and mucosal bleeding develop ^^
 
4.8 Immune Thrombocytopenic Purpura (ITP) ^^
ITP is autoimmune platelet destruction. ^^
Mechanism:
Autoantibodies bind platelets
→ splenic macrophages destroy platelets
→ thrombocytopenia develops
→ bleeding risk increases
Common findings include:
•	petechiae ^^
•	purpura ^^
•	mucosal bleeding
•	low platelet count ^^
Coagulation pathways are usually normal because the problem is platelet destruction rather than clotting-factor deficiency.
 
4.9 Hemophilia ^^
Hemophilia is coagulation-factor deficiency causing impaired secondary hemostasis. ^^
Hemophilia A involves Factor VIII deficiency. ^^
Mechanism:
Factor VIII deficiency
→ intrinsic pathway impaired
→ thrombin generation decreases
→ fibrin formation decreases
→ stable clot formation impaired
→ prolonged bleeding develops ^^
PTT becomes prolonged because the intrinsic pathway is impaired. ^^
Deep tissue bleeding commonly occurs.
Findings may include:
•	hemarthrosis ^^
•	muscle hematomas
•	prolonged bleeding after procedures
Hemarthrosis means bleeding into joints.
 
4.10 von Willebrand Disease ^^
von Willebrand disease involves defective von Willebrand factor. ^^
Normal vWF function:
•	binds collagen
•	mediates platelet adhesion
•	stabilizes Factor VIII
Defective vWF
→ platelet adhesion impaired
→ primary hemostasis impaired
→ bleeding tendency develops
Because vWF stabilizes Factor VIII:
PTT may also become prolonged. ^^
Common findings include:
•	mucosal bleeding ^^
•	epistaxis
•	menorrhagia
•	prolonged bleeding time ^^
 
4.11 Hypercoagulability and Thrombosis ^^
Thrombosis means pathologic clot formation within vessels. ^^
Thrombus formation commonly follows Virchow triad:
•	endothelial injury ^^
•	abnormal blood flow/stasis ^^
•	hypercoagulability ^^
These three factors strongly increase thrombosis risk.
 
4.11A Endothelial Injury ^^
Endothelial injury exposes subendothelial collagen and tissue factor.
Endothelial injury
→ platelet activation develops
→ coagulation activated
→ thrombus formation risk increases
Common causes include:
•	hypertension ^^
•	smoking ^^
•	atherosclerosis ^^
•	trauma
 
4.11B Stasis ^^
Normal blood flow helps prevent clot formation.
Stasis develops when blood flow slows.
Reduced blood flow
→ clotting factors accumulate
→ platelet-endothelium interaction increases
→ thrombosis risk increases
Common causes include:
•	prolonged immobility ^^
•	surgery ^^
•	heart failure
•	atrial fibrillation ^^
 
4.11C Hypercoagulability ^^
Hypercoagulability means increased tendency toward clot formation. ^^
Causes may include:
•	malignancy ^^
•	pregnancy
•	oral contraceptives ^^
•	inherited thrombophilia
•	inflammation
Hypercoagulability
→ coagulation activation increases
→ thrombosis risk increases
 
4.12 Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT) ^^
DVT is thrombus formation within deep veins, usually in the legs. ^^
Mechanism:
Stasis
or
Hypercoagulability
or
Endothelial injury
→ venous thrombus forms
→ venous obstruction develops
→ swelling and pain occur
Common findings include:
•	unilateral leg swelling ^^
•	warmth
•	pain
•	erythema
Major danger:
Venous thrombus dislodges
→ embolus forms
→ pulmonary embolism may occur ^^
 
4.13 Pulmonary Embolism (PE) ^^
Pulmonary embolism occurs when embolic material obstructs pulmonary arteries. ^^
Most pulmonary emboli originate from DVTs.
Mechanism:
DVT develops
→ thrombus dislodges
→ embolus travels through venous circulation
→ embolus reaches pulmonary arteries
→ pulmonary blood flow obstructed
→ ventilation-perfusion mismatch develops
→ hypoxemia develops
Large PE
→ right ventricular strain develops
→ hemodynamic collapse may occur
Common findings include:
•	sudden dyspnea ^^
•	pleuritic chest pain ^^
•	tachycardia
•	hypoxemia
•	hemoptysis
 
4.14 Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC) ^^
DIC involves widespread pathologic coagulation activation. ^^
Mechanism:
Severe systemic trigger develops
→ massive coagulation activation occurs
→ widespread microthrombi form
→ clotting factors consumed
→ platelets consumed
→ secondary bleeding develops
Common triggers include:
•	sepsis ^^
•	severe trauma ^^
•	obstetric complications
•	malignancy
DIC produces both:
•	thrombosis
and
•	bleeding
Microvascular thrombosis
→ organ ischemia develops
Consumption of clotting factors
→ hemorrhage develops
Common findings include:
•	bleeding from multiple sites ^^
•	petechiae ^^
•	organ dysfunction
•	thrombocytopenia ^^
•	prolonged PT/PTT ^^
 
4.15 Anticoagulants and Antiplatelet Drugs ^^
Anticoagulants decrease fibrin formation.
Antiplatelet drugs impair platelet activation.
Heparin enhances antithrombin activity.
Warfarin inhibits vitamin K-dependent clotting-factor synthesis.
Aspirin inhibits thromboxane A2 production.
Reduced thromboxane A2
→ platelet activation decreases
→ platelet aggregation decreases ^^
These medications reduce thrombosis risk but increase bleeding risk.
 
 
5. Shock
Shock is a state of inadequate tissue perfusion causing cellular hypoxia and organ dysfunction. ^^
Normal tissue survival requires:
•	adequate blood volume
•	adequate cardiac output
•	adequate vascular tone
•	adequate oxygen delivery
Shock develops when circulation can no longer maintain sufficient tissue perfusion.
Inadequate perfusion
→ decreased oxygen delivery
→ decreased oxidative phosphorylation
→ decreased ATP production
→ anaerobic metabolism increases
→ lactate production increases
→ cellular dysfunction develops
→ organ failure may occur
Regardless of cause, prolonged shock eventually causes:
•	cellular injury
•	membrane dysfunction
•	inflammatory activation
•	organ failure
•	death
 
5.1 Compensatory Responses in Shock
The body initially attempts compensation.
Decreased tissue perfusion
→ sympathetic nervous system activated
→ catecholamine release increases
→ heart rate increases
→ contractility increases
→ peripheral vasoconstriction develops
Reduced renal perfusion
→ renin release increases
→ RAAS activated
→ sodium and water retention increase
→ blood pressure support attempted
Reduced perfusion also stimulates ADH release.
ADH
→ water retention increases
→ circulating volume partially supported
Early compensation attempts to preserve perfusion to vital organs:
•	brain
•	heart
Peripheral vasoconstriction diverts blood away from skin and extremities.
 
5.2 Stages of Shock
 
5.2A Compensated Shock
Compensation initially maintains blood pressure.
Possible findings include:
•	tachycardia
•	delayed capillary refill
•	anxiety
•	mild oliguria
Cellular hypoxia has begun, but compensation still partially preserves organ perfusion.
 
5.2B Progressive Shock
Compensation begins failing.
Persistent hypoperfusion
→ worsening anaerobic metabolism develops
→ metabolic acidosis worsens
→ myocardial function decreases
→ organ dysfunction progresses
Possible findings include:
•	hypotension ^^
•	confusion
•	oliguria
•	elevated lactate
 
5.2C Irreversible Shock
Severe prolonged hypoperfusion
→ widespread cellular injury develops
→ membrane failure occurs
→ inflammatory cascades amplify
→ multiorgan failure develops
Possible findings include:
•	severe hypotension
•	coma
•	renal failure
•	cardiovascular collapse
At this stage, recovery becomes unlikely even if circulation is restored.
 
5.3 Hypovolemic Shock ^^
Hypovolemic shock develops from severe loss of circulating volume. ^^
Major causes include:
•	hemorrhage ^^
•	severe dehydration
•	burns
Mechanism:
Blood or fluid loss
→ venous return decreases
→ preload decreases
→ stroke volume decreases
→ cardiac output decreases
→ tissue perfusion decreases
→ cellular hypoxia develops
Hemorrhage additionally reduces oxygen-carrying capacity.
Reduced RBC mass
→ oxygen delivery worsens further
→ tissue hypoxia increases
Common findings include:
•	tachycardia
•	hypotension ^^
•	weak pulses
•	oliguria
•	altered mental status
 
5.4 Cardiogenic Shock ^^
Cardiogenic shock develops when the heart cannot pump effectively. ^^
Major causes include:
•	myocardial infarction ^^
•	severe arrhythmia
•	severe heart failure ^^
Mechanism:
Myocardial pump failure develops
→ cardiac output decreases
→ tissue perfusion decreases
→ compensatory vasoconstriction increases afterload
→ myocardial workload worsens
→ shock progresses
In myocardial infarction:
Coronary artery occlusion
→ myocardial ischemia develops
→ ATP depletion occurs
→ myocardial contractility decreases
→ cardiac output falls
→ cardiogenic shock may develop ^^
Left ventricular dysfunction may also produce pulmonary edema.
Left ventricular failure
→ pulmonary venous pressure increases
→ fluid enters alveoli
→ pulmonary edema develops ^^
Common findings include:
•	hypotension ^^
•	dyspnea ^^
•	pulmonary edema ^^
•	jugular venous distention ^^
 
5.5 Distributive Shock ^^
Distributive shock develops from severe pathologic vasodilation. ^^
Mechanism:
Systemic vasodilation develops
→ systemic vascular resistance decreases
→ blood pressure falls
→ tissue perfusion becomes inadequate
Major distributive shock subtypes include:
•	septic shock ^^
•	anaphylactic shock ^^
•	neurogenic shock ^^
Unlike hypovolemic shock:
Circulating volume may initially be normal.
The major problem is loss of vascular tone.
 
5.5A Septic Shock ^^
Septic shock develops from overwhelming systemic infection and inflammatory activation. ^^
Mechanism:
Severe infection develops
→ macrophages activated
→ TNF-alpha and IL-1 release increase
→ nitric oxide production increases
→ widespread vasodilation develops
→ vascular permeability increases
→ hypotension develops ^^
Inflammatory injury also activates coagulation pathways.
Microvascular thrombosis develops
→ tissue perfusion worsens
→ organ dysfunction develops
Increased vascular permeability
→ intravascular fluid leaves circulation
→ relative hypovolemia develops
→ hypotension worsens
Common findings include:
•	fever ^^
•	hypotension ^^
•	elevated lactate
•	multiorgan dysfunction
Septic shock may progress to DIC. ^^
 
5.5B Anaphylactic Shock ^^
Anaphylactic shock is severe systemic Type I hypersensitivity causing distributive shock. ^^
Mechanism:
Systemic allergen exposure
→ widespread mast-cell degranulation occurs
→ histamine release massively increases
→ vasodilation develops
→ vascular permeability increases
→ hypotension develops
→ bronchoconstriction develops
→ airway edema develops ^^
Fluid leaves vessels because permeability increases.
Intravascular volume decreases
→ perfusion worsens
→ shock develops
Common findings include:
•	hypotension ^^
•	urticaria ^^
•	wheezing ^^
•	airway edema ^^
Epinephrine treatment:
Epinephrine
→ vasoconstriction increases blood pressure
→ bronchodilation improves airflow ^^
 
5.5C Neurogenic Shock ^^
Neurogenic shock develops from loss of sympathetic vascular tone. ^^
Major causes include:
•	spinal cord injury ^^
Mechanism:
Sympathetic signaling interrupted
→ vascular tone decreases
→ vasodilation develops
→ blood pressure falls
Unlike most shock states:
Bradycardia may occur. ^^
Reason:
Sympathetic stimulation decreases
→ heart rate fails to compensate appropriately
Common findings include:
•	hypotension ^^
•	bradycardia ^^
•	warm dry skin ^^
 
5.6 Obstructive Shock ^^
Obstructive shock develops when mechanical obstruction impairs circulation. ^^
Major causes include:
•	pulmonary embolism ^^
•	cardiac tamponade ^^
•	tension pneumothorax ^^
 
5.6A Pulmonary Embolism and Obstructive Shock ^^
Large pulmonary embolus
→ pulmonary arterial obstruction develops
→ right ventricular afterload increases
→ right ventricular output decreases
→ left ventricular filling decreases
→ cardiac output decreases
→ shock develops ^^
Common findings include:
•	sudden dyspnea ^^
•	chest pain ^^
•	hypoxemia
•	hypotension ^^
 
5.6B Cardiac Tamponade ^^
Fluid accumulates within pericardial sac
→ cardiac compression develops
→ ventricular filling impaired
→ stroke volume decreases
→ cardiac output decreases
→ obstructive shock develops ^^
Common findings include:
•	hypotension ^^
•	jugular venous distention ^^
•	muffled heart sounds ^^
This combination is Beck triad. ^^
 
5.6C Tension Pneumothorax ^^
Air enters pleural space and cannot escape.
Progressive intrathoracic pressure increase
→ lung collapse develops
→ mediastinum shifts
→ venous return decreases
→ cardiac output decreases
→ obstructive shock develops ^^
Common findings include:
•	hypotension ^^
•	unilateral absent breath sounds ^^
•	tracheal deviation ^^
 
5.7 Multiorgan Failure in Shock
Persistent hypoperfusion
→ cellular injury worsens
→ inflammatory cascades amplify
→ endothelial injury develops
→ microvascular thrombosis develops
→ organ failure progresses
Organs commonly affected include:
•	kidneys
•	lungs
•	liver
•	brain
•	heart
Renal hypoperfusion
→ acute kidney injury develops ^^
Severe prolonged shock
→ multiorgan dysfunction syndrome develops
→ death may occur

6. Chapter 15 — Arterial and Cardiovascular Disorders
Cardiovascular disease develops when blood vessels, blood pressure regulation, coronary perfusion, cardiac muscle function, or cardiac electrical conduction become abnormal.
Major mechanisms include:
•	arterial wall injury
•	plaque formation
•	reduced blood flow
•	myocardial ischemia
•	myocardial infarction
•	pump failure
•	abnormal rhythm
 
6.1 Arteries
Arteries carry blood away from the heart under high pressure.
Normal arteries require:
•	elastic vessel walls
•	intact endothelium
•	smooth muscle regulation
•	open lumen
•	normal blood pressure
Arterial disease develops when the vessel wall becomes damaged, stiffened, narrowed, inflamed, or weakened.
 
6.2 Arteriosclerosis ^^
Arteriosclerosis means hardening or stiffening of arteries. ^^
Mechanism:
Chronic arterial injury
→ vessel wall thickening develops
→ elasticity decreases
→ arterial compliance decreases
→ systolic pressure rises
→ cardiac workload increases
Stiffer arteries cannot expand normally during systole.
Reduced arterial elasticity
→ increased resistance to forward blood flow
→ increased afterload
→ increased strain on the left ventricle
 
6.3 Atherosclerosis ^^
Atherosclerosis is plaque formation within arterial walls. ^^
Atherosclerosis is a type of arteriosclerosis.
Mechanism:
Endothelial injury
→ LDL enters arterial wall
→ LDL becomes oxidized
→ macrophages ingest oxidized LDL
→ foam cells form
→ fatty streak develops
→ smooth muscle cells migrate
→ collagen and extracellular matrix deposited
→ fibrous plaque forms
→ arterial lumen narrows
As plaque enlarges:
Plaque growth
→ lumen narrowing
→ blood flow decreases
→ tissue oxygen delivery decreases
→ ischemia may develop ^^
 
6.4 LDL, HDL, and Plaque ^^
LDL contributes to plaque buildup in arteries. ^^
Mechanism:
High LDL cholesterol ^^
→ increased LDL entry into damaged endothelium
→ LDL oxidation
→ macrophage foam-cell formation
→ fatty streak formation
→ plaque progression
→ atherosclerosis risk increases ^^
HDL helps remove cholesterol from tissues and vessels.
Mechanism:
HDL participates in reverse cholesterol transport
→ cholesterol removal from peripheral tissues increases
→ plaque progression risk decreases
Poor diet ^^
especially diets high in saturated and trans fats
→ LDL levels may rise
→ plaque formation risk increases ^^
Physical inactivity ^^
→ worsened lipid metabolism
→ endothelial dysfunction risk increases
→ cardiovascular risk increases ^^
Age ^^
→ cumulative endothelial injury increases
→ arterial stiffness increases
→ atherosclerosis risk increases ^^
 
6.5 Hypertension ^^
Hypertension is chronically elevated blood pressure. ^^
Blood pressure depends mainly on:
•	cardiac output
•	systemic vascular resistance
•	circulating blood volume
Mechanism:
Increased vascular resistance
or
increased circulating volume
or
increased cardiac output
→ arterial pressure rises
→ endothelial stress increases
→ arterial injury develops
Persistent hypertension
→ endothelial injury
→ LDL entry into arterial wall increases
→ atherosclerosis accelerates
→ vessel stiffness worsens
→ blood pressure rises further
This creates a cycle:
Hypertension
→ endothelial injury
→ arterial stiffening
→ increased resistance
→ worsening hypertension
Long-term hypertension also affects the heart.
Chronic pressure overload
→ left ventricle pumps against higher afterload
→ myocardial cells enlarge
→ left ventricular hypertrophy develops
Hypertrophy initially helps generate stronger contraction.
Over time:
Left ventricular hypertrophy
→ myocardial oxygen demand increases
→ ventricular stiffness increases
→ diastolic filling worsens
→ heart failure risk increases
 
6.6 Coronary Artery Disease ^^
Coronary artery disease develops when coronary arteries become narrowed by atherosclerotic plaque. ^^
Mechanism:
Coronary atherosclerosis
→ coronary lumen narrows
→ myocardial blood flow decreases
→ oxygen supply to myocardium decreases
→ myocardial ischemia develops ^^
The myocardium extracts a high amount of oxygen at baseline.
Therefore, when oxygen demand rises during exertion, the heart depends on increased coronary blood flow.
If coronary arteries are narrowed:
Exercise or stress
→ myocardial oxygen demand increases
→ coronary blood flow cannot increase enough
→ ischemia develops
→ chest pain occurs
 
6.7 Exercise Stress Test ^^
An exercise stress test evaluates coronary artery function during physical exertion. ^^
Mechanism:
Exercise
→ heart rate increases
→ contractility increases
→ myocardial oxygen demand increases
If coronary arteries are significantly narrowed:
Increased oxygen demand
→ inadequate coronary supply
→ ischemic ECG changes or chest pain may occur
Stress testing is useful because coronary disease may not appear at rest.
At rest:
Oxygen demand may be low enough
→ coronary narrowing may not cause symptoms
During exercise:
Oxygen demand rises
→ narrowed coronary arteries fail to meet demand
→ ischemia becomes detectable
 
6.8 Angina Pectoris ^^
Angina pectoris is chest pain caused by myocardial ischemia. ^^
Mechanism:
Coronary blood flow decreases
→ myocardial oxygen delivery decreases
→ ATP production decreases
→ anaerobic metabolism increases
→ lactic acid and metabolites accumulate
→ pain fibers stimulated
→ chest pain develops
Angina means ischemia without myocardial cell death.
If ischemia resolves before necrosis:
Chest pain improves
→ troponin remains normal
→ myocardial infarction has not occurred
 
6.9 Stable Angina ^^
Stable angina is predictable ischemic chest pain, usually triggered by exertion and relieved by rest or nitroglycerin. ^^
Mechanism:
Fixed coronary plaque narrows artery
→ resting blood flow may be adequate
→ exertion increases myocardial oxygen demand
→ narrowed vessel cannot increase flow enough
→ temporary ischemia develops
→ chest pain occurs
Rest relieves stable angina because:
Rest
→ heart rate decreases
→ contractility decreases
→ myocardial oxygen demand decreases
→ ischemia improves
→ chest pain resolves ^^
Nitroglycerin relieves stable angina because:
Nitroglycerin
→ venodilation
→ venous return decreases
→ preload decreases
→ ventricular wall stress decreases
→ myocardial oxygen demand decreases
→ ischemic pain improves ^^
Stable angina does not usually cause troponin elevation because myocardial necrosis does not occur.
 
6.10 ST Depression
ST depression is commonly associated with subendocardial ischemia.
Subendocardium is the inner layer of myocardium.
It is vulnerable because it is exposed to high intraventricular pressure and receives perfusion mainly during diastole.
Mechanism:
Partial coronary flow limitation
→ inner myocardium receives inadequate oxygen
→ subendocardial ischemia develops
→ repolarization abnormalities occur
→ ST depression may appear
ST depression suggests ischemia but not necessarily full-thickness myocardial injury.
It may be seen with:
•	stable angina during exertion
•	unstable angina
•	NSTEMI
 
6.11 Unstable Angina ^^
Unstable angina is worsening myocardial ischemia, often occurring at rest or with minimal exertion. ^^
Mechanism:
Atherosclerotic plaque becomes unstable
→ plaque rupture or erosion occurs
→ platelet activation develops
→ partially occlusive thrombus forms
→ coronary blood flow suddenly decreases
→ ischemia becomes more severe and less predictable
Pain may occur:
•	at rest ^^
•	with minimal exertion
•	with increasing frequency
•	with increasing duration
Unstable angina is not reliably relieved by rest or nitroglycerin.
Reason:
The problem is no longer only increased oxygen demand over a fixed narrowing.
The problem is acute plaque disruption and thrombosis.
Unstable angina:
Ischemia present
→ no myocardial necrosis
→ troponin remains normal
ECG may show:
•	ST depression
•	T-wave inversion
•	no diagnostic change
 
6.12 Acute Myocardial Infarction ^^
Acute myocardial infarction is myocardial necrosis caused by prolonged ischemia. ^^
Mechanism:
Atherosclerotic plaque ruptures
→ collagen and tissue factor exposed
→ platelets adhere and activate
→ coagulation cascade activates
→ thrombus enlarges
→ coronary artery occlusion worsens
→ oxygen delivery falls severely
→ ATP depletion progresses
→ ion pumps fail
→ calcium accumulates in cells
→ mitochondrial injury worsens
→ sarcolemma ruptures
→ myocardial necrosis occurs
Myocardial infarction differs from angina because infarction involves cell death.
Angina:
ischemia
→ no necrosis
→ troponin normal
MI:
ischemia persists
→ necrosis occurs
→ troponin rises ^^
 
6.13 MI Symptoms ^^
Common myocardial infarction symptoms include:
•	severe crushing chest pain ^^
•	diaphoresis ^^
•	shortness of breath
•	nausea
•	radiation to jaw, neck, shoulder, or left arm
•	Levine’s sign ^^
Levine’s sign refers to a patient clutching the chest with a fist to describe ischemic chest pain. ^^
Mechanism of diaphoresis:
Myocardial ischemia
→ sympathetic nervous system activation
→ catecholamine release
→ sweating develops
Elevated blood pressure may occur early from sympathetic activation, but hypotension may occur if pump failure develops.
 
6.14 Cardiac Troponin ^^
Cardiac troponin is released when myocardial cells are injured or necrotic. ^^
Mechanism:
Myocardial necrosis
→ cell membrane disruption
→ intracellular troponin leaks into blood
→ serum troponin rises ^^
Troponin is more specific for myocardial injury than hematocrit, HDL, or IgA.
Troponin elevation supports myocardial infarction when clinical findings match ischemia.
 
6.15 NSTEMI
NSTEMI usually results from incomplete coronary occlusion with subendocardial necrosis.
Mechanism:
Partially occlusive thrombus
→ severe reduction in coronary blood flow
→ subendocardial ischemia develops
→ myocardial necrosis occurs
→ troponin rises
→ ST elevation is absent
ECG pattern:
NSTEMI
→ ST depression or T-wave inversion may occur
→ no persistent ST elevation
Key distinction:
Unstable angina and NSTEMI may look similar clinically.
Unstable angina:
→ no necrosis
→ normal troponin
NSTEMI:
→ necrosis
→ elevated troponin
 
6.16 STEMI
STEMI usually results from complete coronary occlusion with transmural necrosis.
Mechanism:
Complete coronary thrombotic occlusion
→ full-thickness myocardial ischemia
→ transmural injury develops
→ myocardial necrosis occurs
→ troponin rises
→ ST elevation appears
Transmural means the full thickness of the ventricular wall is injured.
ST elevation reflects acute full-thickness myocardial injury.
STEMI is more likely to require urgent reperfusion.
 
6.17 Myocardial Ischemia vs Myocardial Infarction ^^
Myocardial ischemia means oxygen supply is inadequate for myocardial demand. ^^
Myocardial infarction means tissue death occurred due to prolonged ischemia. ^^
Sequence:
Coronary blood flow decreases
→ ischemia develops
→ ATP decreases
→ contractile dysfunction occurs
→ pain develops
→ prolonged ischemia causes necrosis
→ infarction develops
Ischemia can be reversible.
Infarction is irreversible tissue death.
 
6.18 Myocardial Apoptosis ^^
Myocardial apoptosis is programmed cell death. ^^
It is not the main mechanism of acute MI.
Acute MI is primarily necrosis due to ischemic injury.
Mechanism:
Coronary occlusion
→ severe hypoxia
→ ATP depletion
→ membrane failure
→ necrosis
Apoptosis may occur in some chronic remodeling contexts, but acute MI is defined by ischemic myocardial necrosis.
 
6.19 Tachycardia ^^
Tachycardia means abnormally increased heart rate. ^^
Tachycardia may worsen ischemia because:
Heart rate increases
→ myocardial oxygen demand increases
→ diastolic filling time decreases
→ coronary perfusion time decreases
→ ischemia worsens
Tachycardia may also occur as a compensatory response during pain, anxiety, blood loss, shock, or heart failure.
 
6.20 Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting — CABG ^^
CABG creates new routes for blood flow around coronary artery occlusions. ^^
Mechanism:
Severe coronary artery narrowing or blockage
→ myocardial perfusion impaired
→ graft vessel placed beyond blocked segment
→ blood bypasses occlusion
→ distal myocardial blood flow improves
Multiple bypasses can be done at one time. ^^
CABG can be used to treat severe coronary artery disease, angina, and myocardial infarction risk patterns. ^^
Common graft vessels may include:
•	internal mammary artery
•	radial artery
•	saphenous vein
The purpose is not to dissolve plaque.
The purpose is to route blood around obstructed coronary segments.
 
6.21 Heart Failure ^^
Heart failure is a chronic condition in which the heart cannot pump effectively enough to meet body demands. ^^
Heart failure differs from myocardial infarction.
Heart failure:
→ chronic pump dysfunction
→ inadequate forward flow or congestion
Myocardial infarction:
→ acute ischemic event
→ myocardial necrosis from blocked coronary blood flow ^^
MI can cause heart failure if enough myocardium is damaged.
Mechanism:
Myocardial injury
→ contractility decreases
→ stroke volume decreases
→ cardiac output decreases
→ compensatory mechanisms activate
→ fluid retention and remodeling occur
→ heart failure develops
 
6.22 Orthopnea ^^
Orthopnea means shortness of breath when lying flat. ^^
Mechanism:
Lying flat
→ venous return to heart increases
→ failing left ventricle cannot handle increased preload
→ pulmonary venous pressure rises
→ fluid moves into lung interstitium/alveoli
→ breathing becomes difficult
Orthopnea is a hallmark symptom of heart failure. ^^
 
6.23 Left Ventricular Failure ^^
Left ventricular failure causes pulmonary congestion. ^^
Mechanism:
Left ventricle fails to eject blood effectively
→ blood backs up into left atrium
→ pulmonary venous pressure increases
→ pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure rises
→ fluid enters alveoli
→ pulmonary edema develops
→ gas exchange worsens
Clinical findings include:
•	crackles in lungs ^^
•	dyspnea
•	orthopnea ^^
•	pulmonary edema ^^
Crackles occur because fluid is present in alveoli and small airways.
 
6.24 Right Ventricular Failure ^^
Right ventricular failure causes systemic venous congestion. ^^
Mechanism:
Right ventricle fails to pump blood into pulmonary circulation
→ blood backs up into systemic veins
→ venous pressure increases
→ fluid accumulates in peripheral tissues and abdominal organs
Clinical findings include:

7. Respiratory Disorders
The respiratory system delivers oxygen to blood and removes carbon dioxide from the body.
Normal respiratory function requires:
•	open airways
•	effective ventilation
•	intact alveoli
•	normal pulmonary blood flow
•	adequate gas exchange
•	normal respiratory muscle function
Respiratory disease develops when airflow, gas exchange, pulmonary perfusion, or lung structure becomes abnormal.
 
7.1 Ventilation and Gas Exchange
Ventilation means movement of air into and out of the lungs.
Gas exchange occurs at the alveoli.
Normally:
Air reaches alveoli
→ oxygen diffuses across alveolar membrane
→ oxygen enters pulmonary capillaries
→ hemoglobin binds oxygen
→ oxygen delivered to tissues
At the same time:
Carbon dioxide diffuses from blood
→ enters alveoli
→ exhaled from body
Efficient gas exchange requires matching between:
•	ventilation
and
•	perfusion
 
7.2 Ventilation/Perfusion Matching ^^
Ventilation/perfusion matching means alveolar airflow and pulmonary blood flow must be appropriately coordinated. ^^
Ventilation without perfusion:
→ oxygen enters alveoli
→ little blood available to receive oxygen
→ physiologic dead space develops
Perfusion without ventilation:
→ blood reaches alveoli
→ oxygen cannot adequately enter blood
→ shunting develops
Both situations impair oxygenation.
Ventilation/perfusion mismatch
→ arterial oxygen decreases
→ hypoxemia develops ^^
 
7.3 Hypoxemia ^^
Hypoxemia means decreased oxygen concentration in arterial blood. ^^
Causes include:
•	V/Q mismatch ^^
•	hypoventilation
•	diffusion impairment
•	shunting
•	high altitude

→ cellular hypoxia develops
→ compensatory responses activate

•	tachycardia
•	increased respiratory rate
•	sympathetic activation

→ confusion
→ cyanosis ^^
→ organ dysfunction
→ respiratory failure may develop
 
7.4 Cyanosis ^^

7.7 Emphysema ^^

9.1 Acute Coronary Syndrome Review ^^
Acute coronary syndrome, or ACS, describes acute myocardial ischemia caused by sudden reduction in coronary blood flow. ^^
ACS includes:
•	unstable angina ^^
•	NSTEMI
•	STEMI
•	acute myocardial infarction ^^
General sequence:
Atherosclerotic plaque
→ plaque instability
→ plaque rupture or erosion
→ platelet activation
→ thrombus formation
→ coronary blood flow decreases
→ myocardial ischemia develops
→ angina or myocardial infarction occurs
The difference between unstable angina and MI is myocardial necrosis.
Unstable angina:
Ischemia
→ no myocardial necrosis
→ troponin remains normal
Myocardial infarction:
Persistent ischemia
→ myocardial necrosis
→ troponin rises ^^
 
9.2 PTCA / Angioplasty ^^
Percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty, or PTCA, is a procedure used to open narrowed coronary arteries. ^^
Mechanism:
Coronary artery narrowed by plaque
→ catheter inserted into coronary artery
→ balloon inflated at narrowed site
→ plaque compressed against vessel wall
→ lumen widens
→ coronary blood flow improves
Often, a stent is placed.
Stent placement
→ vessel kept open
→ recurrent narrowing risk decreases
PTCA is different from CABG.
PTCA:
Narrowed vessel
→ opened from inside using balloon/stent
CABG:
Blocked vessel
→ bypassed using graft vessel ^^
 
9.3 CABG vs PTCA ^^
CABG creates a new blood-flow route around blocked coronary arteries. ^^
PTCA opens the narrowed artery directly.
CABG:
Severe coronary obstruction
→ graft vessel attached before and after blockage
→ blood bypasses obstruction
→ downstream myocardium receives blood
PTCA:
Coronary narrowing
→ balloon/stent opens existing vessel
→ blood flows through original artery
Both improve myocardial perfusion.
Neither reverses the entire systemic disease process.
Underlying atherosclerosis still requires risk-factor control.

→ valve structure becomes damaged
Vegetations are infected masses made of:
•	bacteria
•	fibrin
•	platelets
•	inflammatory material

Valve infection
→ leaflet destruction
→ poor valve closure
→ backward blood flow develops
Fragments may embolize.
Vegetation fragment breaks off
→ embolus travels through bloodstream
→ infarction or infection may occur elsewhere
Common risk factors include:
•	damaged valves
•	prosthetic valves
•	IV drug use
•	prior endocarditis

9.8 Systolic vs Diastolic Heart Failure ^^
Heart failure may involve impaired contraction, impaired filling, or both. ^^
Systolic heart failure:
Ventricle cannot contract effectively
→ ejection fraction decreases
→ forward cardiac output decreases
Common causes:
•	myocardial infarction ^^
•	dilated cardiomyopathy
•	chronic ischemic heart disease
Diastolic heart failure:
Ventricle cannot relax or fill properly
→ filling pressure increases
→ congestion develops despite preserved contraction
Common causes:
•	hypertension ^^
•	left ventricular hypertrophy
•	hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
Comparison:
Systolic failure
→ weak pump
Diastolic failure
→ stiff pump
Both can cause pulmonary congestion and dyspnea.
 
9.9 Low-Output vs High-Output Heart Failure
Low-output heart failure occurs when the heart cannot pump enough blood forward.
Mechanism:
Pump dysfunction
→ cardiac output decreases
→ tissue perfusion decreases
→ fatigue, weakness, and organ hypoperfusion occur
Common causes:
•	MI
•	cardiomyopathy
•	hypertension
•	valve disease
High-output heart failure occurs when cardiac output is high but still insufficient for body demand.
Mechanism:
Systemic demand increases
or
systemic vascular resistance falls
→ heart pumps more than normal
→ chronic overload develops
→ heart eventually fails
Possible causes:
•	severe anemia
•	hyperthyroidism
•	sepsis
•	arteriovenous fistula
Key distinction:
Low-output failure
→ pump cannot generate enough output
High-output failure
→ body demand is abnormally high or vascular resistance is too low
 
9.10 Cardiomyopathy Types ^^
Cardiomyopathy means disease of the heart muscle. ^^
Major types include:
•	dilated cardiomyopathy
•	hypertrophic cardiomyopathy ^^
•	restrictive cardiomyopathy
Dilated cardiomyopathy:
Ventricular dilation
→ weak contraction
→ systolic failure develops
Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy:
Myocardial thickening
→ impaired ventricular filling
→ possible outflow obstruction
→ exertional syncope or sudden death risk increases ^^
Restrictive cardiomyopathy:
Stiff ventricular walls
→ impaired filling
→ diastolic failure develops
 
9.11 Venous Insufficiency and Varicose Veins ^^
Venous insufficiency occurs when veins cannot return blood effectively back to the heart. ^^
Normal veins contain valves.
Valves prevent backward flow.
Mechanism:
Valve weakness or vein dilation
→ venous blood pools
→ venous pressure increases
→ veins enlarge and twist
→ varicose veins develop ^^
Varicose veins are dilated tortuous superficial veins. ^^
Common findings:
•	visible enlarged veins
•	leg heaviness
•	aching
•	swelling
•	worse symptoms after prolonged standing
Venous insufficiency can also cause:
Venous pressure increases
→ fluid leaves capillaries
→ edema develops
→ skin changes may occur
→ venous ulcers may develop
 
9.12 DVT Risk Integration ^^
Deep vein thrombosis develops when clot formation occurs in deep veins. ^^
Major risk logic follows Virchow triad:
•	stasis ^^
•	endothelial injury ^^
•	hypercoagulability ^^
Examples:
Hip replacement surgery ^^
→ endothelial injury + immobility
→ DVT risk increases
Fracture ^^
→ vascular injury + immobility
→ DVT risk increases
Pregnancy ^^
→ hypercoagulability + venous compression
→ DVT risk increases
Smoking ^^
→ endothelial injury + hypercoagulability
→ DVT risk increases
Obesity ^^
→ venous stasis + inflammation
→ DVT risk increases
DVT
→ thrombus may embolize
→ pulmonary embolism may develop ^^

9.14 Pneumothorax ^^
Pneumothorax occurs when air enters the pleural space. ^^
Mechanism:
Air enters pleural cavity
→ intrapleural pressure increases
→ lung recoil occurs
→ partial or complete lung collapse develops
→ ventilation decreases
→ hypoxemia may occur ^^
Common findings include:
• sudden dyspnea ^^
• unilateral absent breath sounds ^^
• pleuritic chest pain
• tachycardia

Air enters pleural space
→ air cannot escape
→ intrathoracic pressure progressively increases
→ mediastinal shift develops
→ venous return decreases
→ cardiac output decreases
→ obstructive shock develops ^^
Common findings include:
• hypotension ^^
• tracheal deviation ^^
• unilateral absent breath sounds ^^
• severe respiratory distress

• proper dental hygiene ^^

9.17 Sickle Cell Disease Genetics ^^
